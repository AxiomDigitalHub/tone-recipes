  - q: What should I say if my guitar sounds muddy in the church mix?
    a: >-
      Ask for a high-pass filter around 100 to 120 Hz first, then a small cut in
      the 200 to 400 Hz range if it is still thick. Those are the two moves that
      address low-end buildup, and both are quick on any digital console. Saying
      "muddy" alone leaves the tech guessing between four different bands.
  - q: How do I ask to be louder without sounding demanding?
    a: >-
      Ask for a specific amount in a specific place: "could I get a couple of dB
      in the second half of the last chorus?" A bounded request is easy to say
      yes to, and it tells the tech you understand they are balancing a whole
      mix. "Turn me up" with no boundary reads as a request to be louder than
      everyone, permanently.
  - q: Why won't the sound tech boost my guitar at 3 kHz?
    a: >-
      Because 2 to 4 kHz is where vocal consonants live, and protecting that
      band is how they keep the congregation able to understand the words.
      Guitars are usually cut there, not boosted. If you need to cut through,
      ask about 1 to 2 kHz, ask for level, or change what you are playing.
  - q: Should I ask for a high cut on my guitar?
    a: >-
      It helps if your delays and reverbs are washing over the vocal. A low-pass
      somewhere around 8 kHz on electric guitar takes off the sizzle without
      dulling the note attack. If your tone is harsh rather than washy, the
      problem is more likely at 2.5 to 4 kHz and you should fix that at your own
      rig first.
  - q: When is the right time to talk to the sound tech about my tone?
    a: >-
      At rehearsal, or between services. Never mid-song and never in the ninety
      seconds before a service starts, when they are checking a dozen channels
      and cannot hold a new instruction. If you only get one window, use
      rehearsal and bring one request.
  - q: What if the tech is a volunteer who does not know the console well?
    a: >-

      volunteers can find it. Avoid asking for anything that requires multi-band
      parametric work mid-service, and offer to look at it together at rehearsal

There is a specific kind of frustration that comes from standing on a platform knowing something is wrong with how you sound in the room, and having no useful way to say it. So you walk back to the console after the service and say some version of "I sounded muddy out there," and the person at the board nods, and nothing changes the following week — not because they didn't care, but because you handed them a feeling and they needed a frequency.

Most of what gets written about church mixing is written for the person mixing. It tells them to filter the pad and control the low mids and protect the vocal. Almost none of it is written for the player standing twenty feet away who can hear the problem and cannot touch the console. That's the gap this is for.

## The Phrasebook

Left column is what you notice. Middle column is what to ask. Right column is what it costs you, because every one of these is a trade and you should know which one you are making.

| What you hear | What to ask for | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|
| Thick, boomy, sitting on top of the bass | "Can you high-pass me around 120?" | Almost nothing. Electric guitar has very little you want below there |
| Still thick after that | "Could you take a couple of dB out around 250 to 300?" | Body. If the band is thin that day, this can make you disappear |
| Honky, nasal, like a cupped hand | "Can you dip me around 700?" | Some of what makes you audible on a small PA. Go back to it if you vanish |
| Like an ice pick in the third row | "Can you pull 3k down a little?" | Pick definition. Better fixed at your rig — see below |
| My delays are washing over the vocal | "Can you roll my top off above 8k?" | Air on the reverb tails. Usually worth it in a small room |
| I can't hear where I am in the arrangement | "Can I get a little more of myself in my monitor?" | Nothing in the house mix. This is the safest request on the list |
| I disappear in the big chorus | "Could I get a couple of dB in the last chorus?" | Nothing, if you bound it to a section. Everything, if you ask for it globally |

Two rules make all seven of those land. Ask for **one** at a time, and attach a **direction** — up or down, and roughly how much. A tech who hears "can you dip me around 700, maybe two dB" knows exactly what to do and can undo it in one move if it's wrong. That reversibility is most of why the request gets granted.

## Fix These at Your Own Rig First

Some of what sends guitarists to the console is genuinely ours to solve, and asking someone else to patch it is both slower and more expensive in goodwill than fixing it at the board we're standing behind.

Harshness is the big one. If your tone is brittle through the PA but fine in your headphones, that is a cab or impulse response top end meeting a bright PA, and it belongs to you — there's a full walkthrough of the two bands involved in [why worship tone turns harsh through the PA](/blog/worship-guitar-harsh-through-pa-quick-fix). Low-end buildup is often ours too, especially on a modeler running a full-range cab block into a system that already has subs. And if your sound changes between rehearsal and the service, the room filled up with bodies and the PA is being asked to do something different, which is [its own problem with its own fixes](/blog/modeler-preset-sounds-different-live).

The honest test before you walk to the console: is there a knob on my own rig that would do this? If yes, turn it.

## The Two Requests You Should Never Make

**Do not ask for a boost between 2 and 4 kHz.** I spent a long stretch of Sundays convinced this was the reasonable ask — I wanted to cut through in the choruses, that is the band where cutting through happens, so that is what I asked for. What I eventually learned is that 2 to 4 kHz is where vocal consonants live, and that a good engineer is *already cutting* guitars a little in that range specifically so the congregation can understand the words. I was asking, without knowing it, for the one thing that works directly against the reason we're all there. A tech who tells you no on this is doing their job well.

What actually made me audible was a cut, not a boost — pulling a couple of dB out around 400 Hz, which stopped me from occupying the same thick space as the keys and the low end of the vocal. I got more present by taking something away. That inverts how most of us think about being heard, and it's the single most useful thing in this article.

**Do not ask for the whole list mid-service.** Not because it's rude, though it reads that way from the back of the room, but because it cannot work. Somebody riding a service in real time has no room to hold five new instructions. One request, at a time when they can act on it, beats five they'll forget.

## Why "Turn Me Up" Is the Weakest Thing You Can Say

Because there is no version of it a tech can grant without taking something from someone else.

The house mix is a fixed amount of room. "Turn me up" means "make me louder relative to the vocal and the keys," and the person you're asking has been given the opposite instruction by the person leading. So the answer is usually a nod and no change, which both of you then pretend didn't happen.

Bounded versions of the same request work, though, because they don't threaten the balance:

- "Could I get a couple of dB just in the last chorus?" — a fader move, in one place, easy to try.
- "Can I get more of me in my monitor?" — costs the house nothing at all.
- "Am I actually getting quieter in the choruses, or is that me?" — this is a question, not a request, and it sometimes reveals that a compressor is doing something nobody intended. If you're on in-ears, [how compression behaves in a personal mix](/blog/iem-mix-guitar-compression) is worth understanding before you raise it.

The monitor version is underused. A lot of what players read as "I'm buried in the house" is actually "I can't hear myself well enough to play confidently," and those have completely different fixes.

## When the Room Is the Problem, Not the Mix

Sometimes the answer is that nobody at the console can help you, and knowing that saves everyone a frustrating conversation.

A long, hard-surfaced sanctuary will smear anything with a long decay, which means the reverb and delay settings that sounded considered at home are mud in the building. That's an arrangement decision, not an EQ one — shorter tails, fewer repeats, and a little more space between the notes. A room with a boomy low end will make any guitar with energy below 150 Hz sound thick no matter where the high-pass sits.

And if you play a modeler, some of the correction belongs in your global EQ rather than in the mix, because it should apply to every preset you own rather than being re-fixed per channel per week. [The global EQ guide](/blog/modeler-global-eq-guide) covers the difference between correcting your rig and correcting a room — they are not the same job, and doing the second one in the first place is why some players' tones never travel.

## The Conversation That Actually Works

Bring one thing. Say what you hear, name the band if you can, state the direction, and then let them work. "I think I'm thick in the low mids — could we try a high-pass around 120 and see if that's it?" is a complete, actionable sentence that takes eight seconds to say and four seconds to try.

Then, and this is the part most of us skip: come back the next week and tell them whether it worked. A tech who hears "that high-pass fixed it, thank you" will take your next request seriously for years. That's not a technique. It's just how it goes when two people are trying to serve the same forty minutes.

One last thing worth saying out loud, especially if the person at your console is a volunteer who learned the board six months ago: you are asking for help with your instrument from someone who is also holding the drums, the bass, three vocals and a click track. Ask like that's true, because it is.
