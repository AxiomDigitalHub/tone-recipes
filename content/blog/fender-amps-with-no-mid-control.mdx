Somebody brings me an amp about once a month with the same complaint, phrased about the same way: it sounds great on its own and disappears the second a band starts playing around it. Then they ask where the mid knob is, because they have been reading about how to fix exactly that, and every piece of advice they found assumed they had one.

On most of the amps worth owning from that era, they don't. Not because the knob broke, or because somebody pulled it during a repair in 1978, but because Fender never put one there — and understanding what Fender put there instead changes what you do about the problem.

## Quick Answer: Which Blackface and Silverface Fenders Have a Mid Knob

| Amp | Middle control? | Panel (tone section) |
|---|---|---|
| Twin Reverb | Yes, both channels | Volume, Treble, Middle, Bass |
| Super Reverb | Vibrato channel only | Normal: Volume, Treble, Bass · Vibrato: Volume, Treble, Middle, Bass |
| Deluxe Reverb | No | Volume, Treble, Bass |
| Princeton Reverb | No | Volume, Treble, Bass |
| Princeton (non-reverb) | No | Volume, Treble, Bass |
| Vibrolux Reverb | No | Volume, Treble, Bass (both channels) |
| Pro Reverb, blackface–1976 | No | Volume, Treble, Bass (both channels) |
| Pro Reverb, 1976–1982 | Yes, both channels | Volume, Treble, Middle, Bass, plus Master Volume |
| Champ | No | Volume, Treble, Bass |

Two things in that table surprise people. The first is how short the "yes" column is — the Middle knob was the exception in that catalog, not the rule. The second is the Super Reverb, where the knob exists on one channel and not the other. Plug into the Normal inputs of a Super and you are playing a two-knob tone stack, same as a Deluxe Reverb, which is worth knowing before you spend a set wondering why the amp sounds different than it did last week.

## The Knob Isn't Missing. It's a Resistor.

In the amps with the control, the Middle pot is wired as a variable resistor between the bottom of the tone stack and ground. In the amps without it, that same spot in the circuit holds a fixed 6.8k resistor. One part, two leads, soldered in and forgotten for sixty years.

So the circuit is not simpler and it is not missing a stage. It has the same tone network the Twin has. What it lacks is a shaft coming through the faceplate.

Which raises the question everybody skips: if the resistor is standing in for a knob, where is that knob set?

## What 68 Percent Actually Means

Here is the part I had wrong for longer than I would like to admit. I assumed the fixed resistor was Fender's way of saving a dollar on a pot and that it landed the amp somewhere near the scooped end of the range — that a Deluxe Reverb was, roughly speaking, a Twin with the Middle rolled off. Add the pot back, I figured, and you'd finally hear mids the amp had been hiding.

It's the other way around. A 6.8k resistor is 68 percent of a 10k Middle pot's resistance, and the factory voicing on a Deluxe Reverb is therefore nowhere near the mid knob off. On a linear pot that works out to about 7 on the dial.

One caveat on that dial number, because it depends on a part spec that is not settled. Rob Robinette's own Twin Reverb layout drawing labels both Middle pots **10K audio taper**, not linear, and plenty of amp-building references call them linear. The resistance figure is unaffected — 6.8k of 10k is 68 percent either way — but the *dial position* is not. An audio-taper pot reaches a given fraction of its resistance later in the rotation than a linear one, which would put the equivalent setting higher than 7 rather than lower. Every conclusion below survives that either way, and the mid-forward reading gets stronger, not weaker. If you are matching a Twin to a Deluxe by ear, start around 7 and expect to end up above it.

That reframes the whole project. If you install a 10k pot in place of that resistor, nearly everything you have gained is the ability to make the amp *thinner* than it left the factory, with a sliver of new territory above stock. All the range is on the wrong side of where you already were. Which is why the mod disappoints people who expected it to hand them midrange, and why the players who are happy with it are the ones who wanted a scoop control — a way to get out of a vocalist's way on a rhythm part, and then step back in.

And if the resistor is already sitting at 7, the mids that go missing when the band starts up are not the resistor's doing.

## Where the Mids Went

They went into the tone stack, and they go there on every one of these amps, knob or no knob.

A passive Treble-Middle-Bass network like Fender's does not have independent controls. Everything interacts, and the topology itself takes a bite out of the low mids — a few hundred Hz, moving around as you work the Treble and Bass knobs. On a Deluxe Reverb that notch is fixed in place. On a Twin Reverb it is adjustable, but only in one direction: the Middle control decides how deep the notch goes, and at 10 the circuit is as flat as it is ever going to be. There is no setting that fills it back in.

That is worth sitting with, because it means the mid knob is not a mid *boost*. A Fender at Middle 10 is not adding anything. It is declining to subtract.

You can hear the design intent once you know it's there. A guitar with a hollow at 400 Hz and a lively top end sits under a singer without a fight, which is what these amps were built into a band to do. The scoop is not a flaw they forgot to fix. It's the sound, and it's why a Deluxe Reverb recorded in 1965 still sounds like it's making room for somebody.

There's one more wrinkle in the parts. The Super Reverb, the Concert and the reissue-era '68 Custom Deluxe Reverb use a 0.022 µF capacitor in the mid leg where the rest of the AB763 line uses 0.047 µF. The smaller cap moves the mid control's band higher and makes the scoop shallower — a small change on a parts list, and a real one under your hands.

## The Pro Reverb Problem: Same Name, Two Panels

If you own a Pro Reverb, the answer to "does it have a mid knob" is a question about the year, not the model.

Blackface Pro Reverbs and silverface ones through about 1976 have Volume, Treble and Bass per channel and no Middle. Starting in 1976 the panel changed: Volume, Treble, Middle, Bass on both channels, plus a Master Volume. Two amps, one nameplate, a decade apart.

This matters beyond trivia. Settings advice for "a Pro Reverb" is worthless without the year attached, and a used listing that says Pro Reverb tells you nothing about what you'll be turning. Ask for a photograph of the panel before money moves. That goes double for the silverface years generally, where Fender changed circuits more often than the cosmetics suggest.

## What to Change When the Amp Won't Give You Mids

Nothing in this section requires opening the amp.

| What you want | The move | What it costs you |
|---|---|---|
| Mids in the room, right now | A mid-hump overdrive in front, gain low, level up. A Tube Screamer-style circuit humps around 720 Hz — nearly on top of the notch | It is a drive pedal, so you get some compression and a little grit with the mids. Set the Drive at 1 or 2 and the Level at 7 to keep that minimal |
| The amp to stop sounding boomy before it sounds mid-forward | Bass down one or two positions from where you have it | At bedroom volume the amp gets lean fast. This is a loud-room move |
| A real, cuttable mid control | A graphic or parametric EQ in front, working 400 Hz and 800 Hz | Another box and another cable. In front of the amp it is also hitting the preamp, so a boost there is partly a gain change |
| More apparent mids without touching the amp | Roll the guitar volume to 8 and pick harder; move to the neck-and-bridge blend if you have it | Less top end, which on a bright blackface amp is usually a trade you will take |
| The knob, permanently | Replace the 6.8k resistor with a 25k or 100k linear pot | A hole in the faceplate, a tech's time, and a real conversation about resale if the amp is an original |

The first row is the one that solves the actual complaint most of the time. A mid-hump pedal in front of a Deluxe Reverb is not a workaround for the missing knob — it is a better tool than the knob would have been, because it *adds* at a frequency the tone stack can only subtract at. That is also why that combination became the standard Texas blues rig rather than some accident of what was on the shelf.

## The Mod That Puts the Pot Back

If you want the control, pick the value deliberately.

A 10k pot is the historically correct choice in the sense that it matches what the Twin and Super use, and as established above, it puts your stock sound at roughly 7 or above with everything interesting below it. That is the right pick if what you actually want is a scoop control.

A 25k or 100k linear pot goes further. Past the stock 6.8k the added resistance starts acting as a raw control, boosting signal in a way the fixed resistor never does, and the amp drifts out of blackface territory toward something closer to a tweed voicing. More range, more sounds, and a control that no longer has a "stock" position you can find by feel.

Either way: it is a modification to a circuit that is often the most valuable thing in the room. On a reissue, have at it. On a 1964 amp with its original transformers, the conversation with a good tech should start with whether a pedal gets you 90 percent of the way there for none of the risk. Usually it does.

The Middle knob is not the only control that is missing, or present, depending on which side of the panel you plug into. Which channel carries reverb, tremolo, a Bright switch and a third gain stage varies by model too, and on a Super Reverb the Middle knob itself is on one channel only — [the per-channel breakdown is here](/blog/fender-normal-vs-vibrato-channel).

## If You're Playing the Model Instead of the Amp

Every modeler's blackface Deluxe has a Mid knob, and none of the real ones do.

That is not a bug — the model makers added a post-model tone control because people expect one. But it means the neutral position is not zero, and the modeler is not showing you the amp until you find it. On Line 6's US Deluxe Vib, the invented Mid control is neutral at noon, which is the single most common reason a model "doesn't sound like the amp" to someone who knows the amp. Our [Deluxe Reverb settings guide](/blog/fender-deluxe-reverb-settings) walks through the stock voicing on both the real panel and the model, and [the Tone Master comparison](/blog/fender-deluxe-reverb-vs-tonemaster) covers what changes when the amp itself is doing the modeling.

There is a genuine advantage in the digital version, and it isn't the extra knob. It's that you can put an EQ block *after* the amp and cab, which no 1965 amp allows without an effects loop it doesn't have. A narrow lift of a couple of dB around 500 Hz placed there gives you the mids the tone stack removed, without pushing the preamp harder — the one move in this entire article that the original circuit genuinely cannot do. If you want the full version of that approach, [the modeler EQ guide](/blog/modeler-eq-guide) has the block placement, and [the EQ pedal placement piece](/blog/eq-pedal-placement) covers the analog equivalent for a board.

For everyone playing the real thing: the knob isn't coming. Learn where the notch sits, put something in front of the amp that fills it, and quit turning a Bass control hoping it will do a Middle control's job. Sixty years of players got that sound out of three knobs. Two, if we're being honest about it.
