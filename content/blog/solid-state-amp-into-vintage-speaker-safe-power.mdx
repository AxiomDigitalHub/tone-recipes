The amplifiers these speakers were built for ran out of breath early. A tweed Deluxe, a blackface Deluxe Reverb, a Vox AC15: these were amps measured in tens of watts, and the speakers bolted into them (the Jensen P10Rs and P12Rs, the alnico Concert series, the early Oxfords) were rated to match. Then came the 1970s, the big solid-state heads and combos, the steel players and keyboard players who wanted clean volume without end, and a lot of those old speakers ended up hanging off amplifiers that could deliver four, six, eight times what the speaker was ever asked to take.

Some of them are still fine, and some of them are reconed, and the difference usually isn't the wattage printed on the amp. It's which way the trouble came in.

## What the Watt Rating on a Speaker Actually Measures

A speaker's power rating is a heat number. Eminence, which publishes its method plainly, rates its speakers under EIA 426A: the speaker is tested in free air with a continuous noise signal at a 6 dB crest factor, and the company calls the result "basically a thermal limit." In other words, the rating tells you how much average power the voice coil can turn into heat, all day, without cooking its glue or its former.

Jensen publishes two figures for its vintage-alnico line and doesn't publish the method behind either. The P12R and P10R are each listed at 25 watts rated and 50 watts "musical power," and the P12Q at 40 and 80. The musical figure is always exactly double, which matches how Eminence describes its own music program rating: "always twice the continuous rating," higher because music has peaks and dips and isn't as abusive as continuous noise. The lower number is the one to plan around.

Jensen's own guideline for matching is simple: total cabinet wattage, which is the sum of the speakers, should be about twice the amplifier's output.

| Speaker (Jensen vintage alnico) | Rated power | "Musical power" | Amp by Jensen's 2× guideline |
|---|---|---|---|
| P10R | 25 W | 50 W | about 12 W per speaker |
| P12R | 25 W | 50 W | about 12 W per speaker |
| P12Q | 40 W | 80 W | about 20 W per speaker |
| C12N (ceramic, for comparison) | 50 W | — | about 25 W per speaker |

That's a conservative rule, and plenty of single-speaker tube combos have run hotter than it for sixty years. But it's the maker's number, and it's worth knowing which side of it you're on before the next part.

## Three Ways a Vintage Speaker Gets Hurt

There are three separate failures, and the amp type changes which one is likely.

1. **Heat.** The voice coil overheats from too much *average* power. The glue softens, the former warps, the coil rubs or opens. This follows how loud you play and how hard the amp clips.
2. **Travel.** The cone moves farther than the suspension and coil were built for. The coil can leave the gap and come back crooked, the former can strike the back plate, the spider or surround can tear. This follows the amp's *peak voltage* and the frequency of the note.
3. **Magnet.** On alnico speakers, a big enough current spike in the voice coil can knock down the magnet's flux. That costs sensitivity permanently until the magnet is recharged. This follows peak *current*, which at low frequencies runs close to voltage divided by the coil's DC resistance.

I expected the danger with a big solid-state amp to be heat, because that's what the watt rating describes and that's what the forum rule of thumb ("don't put 200 watts into a 25-watt speaker") assumes. The published numbers point at the other two.

## Why a Cranked Tube Amp Is Harder on Heat

A tube amp's rating is its clean output, measured on a sine wave just short of clipping. Turn it up past that and the waveform flattens, and a hard-clipped wave approaches a square wave. A square wave at the same peak voltage carries twice the average power of a sine, because it sits at full voltage the whole time instead of passing through zero. Guitar.com's amp FAQ puts a guitar amp's maximum output at up to 90 percent above its clean rating, which lands in the same place.

So a 30-watt tube amp, cranked all night, can push something like 55 or 60 watts of average power into its speakers. That's the condition the old "speaker breakup" sound comes from, and it's why a dimed AC30 or Deluxe goes through speakers.

A clean solid-state amp kept below clipping does the opposite. Its rating is a ceiling it only touches on the loudest peaks, and guitar, steel and keys all carry far less average power than their peaks. Played at the same loudness as the tube amp, the big clean amp is usually delivering *less* heat to the coil, not more. The heat risk comes back only when someone runs it into its own clip light, at which point it is a very large amp making a square wave.

## Why Cone Travel Is the Real Limit

Cone travel doesn't care about average power. It follows the voltage across the coil from instant to instant, and below the speaker's resonance it follows it almost directly: force is the motor strength times the current, and the cone moves until the suspension's stiffness pushes back.

Jensen publishes enough of its Thiele-Small figures (force factor BL, coil resistance Re, suspension compliance Cms, and Xmax) to work that through. Here is the low-frequency cone travel for the 8-ohm versions, in free air, which is about what an open-back combo gives the cone:

| Speaker (8 Ω) | Xmax (Jensen) | Fs (Jensen) | Power to reach Xmax, below resonance | Peak travel at 25 W | Peak travel at 100 W |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P10R | ±0.8 mm | 97 Hz | about 1.7 W | about 3.1 mm | about 6.2 mm |
| P12R | ±1.0 mm | 80 Hz | about 3.0 W | about 2.9 mm | about 5.7 mm |
| P12Q | ±1.0 mm | 90.5 Hz | about 4.3 W | about 2.4 mm | about 4.8 mm |

Calculated from Jensen's published BL, Re and Cms as BL × (V / Re) × Cms, with power taken as V² across the nominal 8 Ω. At resonance itself the cone moves further still, by roughly the speaker's total Q, which Jensen lists at 1.6 to 2.1 for these three.

Two cautions keep that table honest. Xmax is the *linear* limit, the point where the coil starts leaving the even part of the magnetic field, and it isn't the damage limit. Guitar speakers go past it routinely, and the distortion they make there is part of the sound. The number that would tell you where damage starts, the mechanical limit, isn't on Jensen's sheet, and I couldn't find it published for any of these speakers. The second caution is that the table is free air. A sealed cabinet adds an air spring behind the cone and cuts low-frequency travel.

Even so, the table makes the shape of the problem clear. These speakers have small motors and short coils, and they're past Xmax at single-digit watts on low notes. Every doubling of power adds another 41 percent of travel, and what limits the travel in practice is how much voltage the amp can deliver on a peak.

| Amplifier | Rating | Clean voltage at rated power |
|---|---|---|
| A 25-watt combo, 8 Ω | 25 W | about 14.1 V |
| A 30-watt tube amp, 8 Ω | 30 W | about 15.5 V |
| '65 Twin Reverb reissue | 85 W @ 4 Ω | about 18.4 V |
| Peavey Nashville 400 | 210 W RMS @ 4 Ω | about 29.0 V |

A solid-state amp behaves roughly like a voltage source, so the Nashville 400 can put close to 29 volts across an 8-ohm speaker on a peak, delivering a bit over 100 watts into it. That's about twice the voltage of a 25-watt amp, which means about twice the cone travel on the same low note. The small tube amp simply runs out: past its rating it clips, and clipping caps the voltage. That's the old amp protecting the old speaker, and it's the protection a clean solid-state amp doesn't give you.

## Where the Low Notes Land

The travel is greatest at resonance, and resonance on these speakers sits right among the notes guitar players actually use. Jensen lists the 8-ohm P12R at 80 Hz. A guitar's open low E is 82 Hz. A pedal steel's E9 low B is 123 Hz, a drop-D guitar goes to 73 Hz, a bass guitar's open E is 41 Hz, and a keyboard's left hand goes lower still.

Eminence makes the same point from the pro-audio side: "bass frequencies are more abusive," and in low-frequency work "it might actually take a 500 watt woofer to handle 300 watts of amplifier power." The company also says the mechanical-versus-thermal question isn't significant for ordinary lead and rhythm guitar cabinets, and that holds true for ordinary guitar. The trouble starts when the vintage speaker is fed something that isn't ordinary guitar: a steel with the bass up (our [pedal steel headroom guide](/blog/pedal-steel-amp-headroom-how-much-clean-power) covers why that's where steel rigs spend their power), a baritone, a keyboard, or a bass knob on 10 through a 200-watt amp. An open-back combo makes it worse, because the low end cancels around the cabinet and the natural response is to turn it up.

## The Magnet, for Alnico Owners

The third failure is the one people don't see coming, and it rides on the same peaks. JBL's Greg Timbers, writing about the company's alnico drivers, said the motors could be demagnetized by drive, costing up to about 3 dB of level permanently. He also said the 15 to 30 watt tube amps those speakers were designed around couldn't hurt them, and the problem arrived with big solid-state amps. At low frequencies the coil's current is set mostly by its DC resistance, so the same bass-heavy peaks that drive the cone farthest also push the most current through the coil. Our piece on [whether alnico magnets weaken and what a recharge restores](/blog/alnico-speaker-magnet-recharge-does-it-weaken) has the full story.

## So What Is Actually Safe

| Your rig | What to do |
|---|---|
| Vintage 25 W alnico in its original low-wattage tube combo | Nothing to change. The amp clips before it can over-travel the cone. Watch the heat only if it lives on 10. |
| Same speaker, clean solid-state amp of 100 W or more, ordinary guitar | Fine at band volume. Keep the amp out of clipping, and keep the bass knob below noon on the low strings. |
| Same speaker, steel, baritone, keys or bass, through a big clean amp | High-pass at about 80 to 100 Hz, or turn the bass well down. Better: use a modern high-power driver for the job and keep the vintage one for the tube amp. |
| Two vintage speakers sharing the load | Each needs less drive for the same loudness. Follow Jensen's 2× guideline on the total. |
| Open-back combo with a lot of low end demanded of it | Consider a closed back for that use. Our guide to [converting an open-back combo to closed-back](/blog/convert-open-back-combo-to-closed-back) covers the job. |
| Any speaker that starts to flap, buzz or rattle on low notes | Stop and turn down. That noise is the cone reaching the end of its travel. |

Three habits do most of the work.

1. **Match on Jensen's guideline when you can.** Total speaker rating of about twice the amp's output is the maker's number, and it keeps you clear of heat in every case.
2. **When you can't, manage the peaks.** On a big clean amp, the master volume and the bass control matter more than the wattage on the back panel. If the amp has a clip or limiter light, it shouldn't be lighting.
3. **Take the deep lows away from small vintage motors.** The amp's bass knob, a low-cut on an EQ pedal, or a high-pass in front of the power amp all do it. Set the cut near the speaker's resonance, around 80 Hz for a P12R and closer to 100 Hz for a P10R.

## If You Play Through a Modeler

The same physics applies to a modeler feeding a power amp into a real cabinet. A Helix, Quad Cortex or Kemper running into a 200-watt class-D power amp and a vintage 2x12 is the big clean solid-state amp in every way that matters to the speaker. Put a low cut at the end of the chain or in the global output EQ, about 80 to 100 Hz, and keep the power amp out of clipping.

The other route skips the risk. If what you want is the sound of a vintage P12R behind a powerful rig, an impulse response of that speaker through a full-range cab rated for the power gets you the speaker's voice without asking a 25-watt motor to take a 200-watt peak. For what a real cabinet passes at the bottom anyway, see [what a guitar cab actually passes in the low end](/blog/guitar-speaker-low-end-rolloff-what-a-cab-actually-passes), and for wattage matching in a new cabinet, our [speaker power handling guide](/blog/speaker-power-handling-watts-cab).

The old speakers have outlived most of the amplifiers that came after them. They earned it by being used the way they were designed, in front of amps that ran out of voltage long before they did. A big clean amp can respect that if you take the bottom octave away from it.
