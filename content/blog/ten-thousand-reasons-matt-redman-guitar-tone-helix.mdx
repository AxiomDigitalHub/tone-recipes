*10,000 Reasons (Bless the Lord)* sits at 72.5 BPM, and that number sets a trap for the electric guitar tone. A tempo that slow usually means long reverb and a wide delay, the kind of wash a ballad can hold. This song can't hold it, because the chords don't move slowly. The chart puts two chords in every bar, so the harmony turns over every two beats, and the delay and reverb have to be set for that, not for the click.

| Recording | Key | BPM | Length | One chord (2 beats) | Dotted 1/8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Matt Redman, *10,000 Reasons* (2011) | G | 72.5 | 5:46 | 1.66 s | 621 ms |
| Maranatha! Music, *Worship Solutions Volume Two* | G | 72.5 | 4:21 | 1.66 s | 621 ms |
| Jesus Culture, *Unstoppable Love* (2014) | C | 70 | 7:15 | 1.71 s | 643 ms |
| The Maker & The Instrument, *Instrumental Songs of Worship* (2019) | A | 64 | 4:47 | 1.88 s | 703 ms |
| Stephen McWhirter, *Unto The Lord* (2025) | A | 60 | 4:12 | 2.00 s | 750 ms |
| Rend Collective, *iWorship Now Next 2013* | E (PraiseCharts) | not published | — | — | — |

Keys, tempos and lengths for the first five rows are from MultiTracks; all are in 4/4. Matt Redman and Jonas Myrin wrote the song (CCLI 6016351), it draws on Psalm 103, and it won two Grammys in 2013. PraiseCharts tags the original "Med Slow" in G with the style "Celtic," and lists the 10th Anniversary recording in Db. WorshipTogether gives the original key as C, though the chart on that page uses C, G, D and Em, which are G-major chords. The song begins on the IV chord, which is probably where the C comes from. If your chart says C at the top and the chords look like G, you have the right chart.

## Two Chords Per Bar Changes the Math

WorshipTogether charts the intro as C, G, D/F#, Em, then C, G, Dsus, D, with two beats on each. The chorus and verse lines run through the same chords in the same order. At 72.5 BPM a beat lasts 828 ms, so each chord gets 1.66 seconds before the next one arrives.

I expected a 72.5 BPM song to have room for the biggest reverb in the set. The chord chart says otherwise. A song at 145 BPM that changes chords once per bar gives each chord the same 1.66 seconds, and nobody would put a 4-second hall on that. The tempo is slow; the harmony isn't.

Here is what a long tail does at this pace. Reverb decay is the time the tail takes to fall 60 dB. With a 4-second decay, the tail has dropped only about 25 dB when the next chord arrives, so the D chord's F# and A are still plainly audible under the Em, and a third chord arrives before the first has gone. With a 1.7-second decay the tail is about 58 dB down at the change. Each chord clears as the next one starts. The [reverb decay versus tempo guide](/blog/reverb-decay-vs-song-tempo-when-tails-collide) has the general rule; this is that rule applied to this chart.

The delay follows the same rule. A dotted eighth at 72.5 BPM is 621 ms, so repeats land 621 ms and 1,241 ms after each strum, both inside the chord. A third repeat would arrive at 1,862 ms, about 200 ms into the next chord. At 20 percent feedback that third repeat is too quiet to matter. At 45 percent it isn't.

There's a low-end reason to stay tidy too. The intro's D/F# puts a walking line in the bass: G, F#, E. Keep the electric's low cut high enough that a ringing low string doesn't blur that line for the bass player and the pianist's left hand.

## The Helix Preset

Set the preset tempo to 72.5 for the Redman recording, or let the click drive it over [MIDI clock](/blog/helix-midi-clock-playback-click-worship). The delay is a note value, so the same preset follows a 70 or 60 BPM version.

| Block | Settings | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Volume Pedal** | First in the chain | Swells for the intro and breakdown; the delay and reverb ring on after you back off |
| **Deluxe Comp** | Threshold −22 dB, Ratio 3:1, Mix 50% | Evens out picked two-note shapes without flattening a strum |
| **Minotaur** | Gain 3, Tone 5, Level 6; off until the instrumental | A mid-forward push for the build and the double chorus |
| **Matchstick Ch1** | Drive 3.5, Bass 4, Mid 6, Treble 5.5, Ch Vol to taste | Clean with a little give when you dig in |
| **2x12 Match H30** | Low Cut 110 Hz, High Cut 7 kHz | Low cut stays clear of the G, F#, E bass walk; high cut keeps in-ears from getting brittle |
| **Transistor Tape** | Time 1/8 dotted, Feedback 20%, Mix 16% | Two audible repeats, both inside the chord |
| **Plateaux** | Decay 1.7 s, Mix 22%, Low Cut 200 Hz | One chord long at 72.5 BPM |

For a 60 BPM arrangement, each chord lasts 2 seconds, so the Plateaux decay can come up to about 2 seconds and still clear.

## Four Snapshots for Redman's Arrangement

MultiTracks maps the original as Intro, Chorus, Verse 1, Chorus, Verse 2, Chorus, Instrumental, Breakdown, Verse 3, Chorus, Chorus, Tag, Tag, Outro, Tag, Tag, Tag, Ending. Two things stand out. The song opens on the chorus, so the first chorus is the quietest one and can't be your full chorus sound. And the last third is five tags around an outro: the song comes down slowly, and the electric needs somewhere to go besides off.

| Snapshot | Where | Changes |
|---|---|---|
| 1: Open | Intro, first chorus, verse 1, breakdown, verse 3 | Minotaur off, delay 20%, Plateaux 28% at 1.7 s; play swells with the volume pedal |
| 2: Chorus | Choruses after verses 1 and 2 | Minotaur off, delay 16%, Plateaux 22% |
| 3: Build | Instrumental, double chorus after verse 3 | Minotaur on, delay 16%, Plateaux 20% |
| 4: Tags | Tags, outro, ending | Minotaur off, delay 22%, Plateaux 30%, decay 3 s |

Snapshot 4 is the only place the decay goes long, and it earns it at the ending, where the last chord resolves from Gsus to G and holds. Through the tags themselves, play less than you think you need to: one swell per chord, and let the 3-second tail carry it. If the tags feel cloudy in rehearsal, pull the decay back to 2 seconds and keep the mix.

Verse 3 comes straight out of a breakdown. Use snapshot 1 there, play nothing for the first half, and swell in on the second. The double chorus that follows will sound bigger for it than any amount of added gain.

Other versions need small changes. Jesus Culture's 7:15 arrangement adds repeated choruses, two instrumentals and a pair of vamps before the outro; snapshot 3 covers the instrumentals and snapshot 4 the vamps. Stephen McWhirter's map is chorus, turnaround, verse three times with no closing chorus, so snapshots 1 and 2 are the whole song.

## Other Platforms

- **HX Stomp:** use your guitar's volume knob for the swells and drop the Volume Pedal block. Comp, Minotaur, amp, cab, Transistor Tape and Plateaux are six blocks.
- **Quad Cortex:** a clean-to-edge Matchless or AC-style capture, a Klon-type drive in front, a tape delay at a dotted eighth, and a plate with the decay set near 1.7 seconds. Use scenes for the four states above.
- **Pedalboard:** tap the quarter note and select the dotted-eighth division, with feedback low enough to hear two repeats. On the reverb, set the decay by ear: strum the D, change to Em, and turn the decay down until you can't hear the D under it.

## Before Rehearsal

Ask which recording the team learned, then read your own chart for the chord pace. If your arrangement holds one chord per bar anywhere, such as a long vamp, that's 3.3 seconds per chord and the longer decay is safe there. Everywhere else, one chord of reverb is enough.
