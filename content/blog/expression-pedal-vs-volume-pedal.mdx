> **The direct answer:** A hardware volume pedal is a pot in your audio path. In front of a modeler it changes the input level every block sees, and it loads your pickups. An expression pedal never touches the audio. It moves a parameter inside the modeler, usually a volume block that you can put wherever you want in the chain. If you want amp clean-up with no treble cost, an expression pedal driving a volume block *before* the amp gets you both.

| What you want | Best tool | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| Pull back to clean up the amp, like the guitar volume knob | Expression pedal → Volume block | Before the amp block |
| Same, with no setup and no modeler at all | Passive volume pedal | Between guitar and amp input, ideally after a buffer |
| Swells that keep delay and reverb tails ringing | Expression pedal → Volume block | After amp/drives, before delay and reverb |
| One pedal that does volume in one preset and wah or reverb mix in another | Expression pedal | Assign per preset |
| Level control on a pedalboard with no modeler | Passive volume pedal (250k after passive pickups, 25k after a buffer) | Anywhere, but see the tone cost below |

The question comes up constantly in modeler forums: do you need a dedicated hardware volume pedal, or can an expression pedal assigned to a volume block inside the Helix, HX Stomp or Quad Cortex do the same job? They aren't equivalent, and the differences are specific: where the level change happens, what it does to your pickups, and which jacks they go in.

---

## How Each Approach Works

### Hardware Volume Pedal

A passive volume pedal is a potentiometer in the audio path, usually between the guitar and the modeler's input. Moving it changes the resistance your signal passes through. There's no processing and nothing to update; the level changes as the pot turns.

Because it sits before the modeler, every block sees the new level. Backing off has the same effect as rolling back the guitar's volume: the amp and drive models get a weaker signal and clean up.

### Expression Pedal (Controlling a Volume Block)

An expression pedal is also a pot, commonly 10k linear (Mission Engineering's SP1-ND for the Quad Cortex lists a 10k linear pot), but it's wired to a control jack, not to the audio. Most modelers put a small reference voltage across it on a TRS cable and read where the wiper sits; Line 6 reads its resistance on a plain TS cable instead, which is why pot value matters more on a Helix (see [expression pedal pot values by platform](/blog/expression-pedal-pot-value-10k-25k-50k-modeler)). That position moves whatever parameter you've assigned: the level of a Volume Pedal block, a wah's sweep, a reverb's mix.

Your guitar's audio never goes through it. The pedal itself does nothing to your tone. The tone change comes from **where you put the block it controls.**

---

## The Tone Cost a Passive Pedal Brings With It

This is the difference most comparisons skip. A passive volume pedal in front of a modeler costs treble in two separate ways, and an expression pedal costs neither.

**1. Partway down, it adds series resistance.** A pot's output resistance peaks at a quarter of its value: 62.5k for a 250k pedal. On a standard audio taper that peak lands around 70 to 75 percent of the travel, not halfway. That resistance and the capacitance of the cable after the pedal form a low-pass filter. With a short patch into a modeler's buffered input it sits far above the guitar's range. With a long cable after the pedal it can fall to about 3 kHz. The full breakdown is in [why a volume pedal dulls partway down](/blog/volume-pedal-treble-loss-halfway-why).

**2. At full up, it's an extra load on your pickups.** A guitar with 250k volume and tone pots into a 1 MΩ input sees about 111k. Add a 250k volume pedal and the guitar sees about 77k. On a typical passive single coil with 15 ft of cable, that moves the resonant peak from about 3,520 Hz down to about 3,020 Hz. If you also use the pedal's tuner out, the tuner's cable adds more; we worked through that in [what a volume pedal's tuner out costs](/blog/volume-pedal-tuner-out-tone-loss-does-it-load).

Both costs disappear if a buffer comes before the pedal, or if the "volume pedal" is an expression pedal driving a block.

## The Practical Differences

### Gain Structure Response

With a hardware volume pedal before the modeler, pulling back reduces the level every block receives. An amp model set at the edge of breakup cleans up as you back off.

With an expression pedal, it depends on where the Volume block sits:

- **Before the amp block:** same clean-up behavior as a hardware pedal, because the amp model gets less input. No pickup loading, no partway-down treble loss.
- **After the amp and drives, before delay and reverb:** the amp's character stays constant and only the level drops. Your delay and reverb tails keep ringing when you pull back to nothing, which is what most swell players want.
- **At the end of the chain:** everything drops together, tails included. Useful as a master volume, less useful for swells.

That placement choice is the real advantage of the expression approach. A hardware pedal in front of the modeler can only do the first option.

### Taper

A passive volume pedal's taper is set by its pot, almost always audio (logarithmic) taper, which makes the volume change feel even across the travel. Some pedals add a taper switch; Ernie Ball's VP Jr. has one.

Expression pedals are usually linear, and the modeler maps the travel to a parameter range. On Helix and HX Stomp you set Min and Max values per assignment; on the Quad Cortex you calibrate each EXP input's range in I/O settings. The Volume Pedal block on Helix also has its own taper setting. You get more control over feel, at the cost of a few minutes of setup.

### Latency

A passive pedal changes level as the pot moves. An expression pedal is read by the modeler and applied at its control rate. In practice, for swells and level rides, this isn't the deciding factor; the placement and loading differences above matter far more.

## Which Jacks Do What

None of these units pass audio through their EXP jacks. A passive TS volume pedal plugged into an EXP jack will not act as a volume control.

**Helix Floor:** the built-in treadle covers EXP 1, and its toe switch toggles it to EXP 2 when no external pedal is connected. The rear panel has **EXP 2 and EXP 3** jacks for external expression pedals (Line 6 and Mission's L6 models use a TS cable, 10k linear); plug a pedal into EXP 2 and the built-in treadle stays on EXP 1. A new Volume Pedal block defaults to EXP 2, and a Wah defaults to EXP 1, per Line 6's manual as quoted by users.

**Helix LT:** same idea, with a built-in treadle and rear EXP 2 and EXP 3 jacks.

**HX Stomp:** one **EXP 1/2 FS 4/5** jack (TRS). Line 6's manual says a Y-cable (TRS split to two TS) lets you connect two expression pedals; set the tip and ring functions in Global Settings. A passive volume pedal goes in front of the Stomp's input, in the audio path, not in this jack.

**Quad Cortex:** **EXP 1 and EXP 2** TRS inputs, calibrated in I/O settings. Neural DSP's support article also covers splitting a dual-output pedal across both so the toe switch can bypass a block.

**Pedalboard with no modeler:** you need a passive (or active) volume pedal; there's nothing for an expression pedal to talk to. Use a 250k pedal if passive pickups hit it first, a 25k pedal if it comes after a buffer, and keep the cable after it short.

## When to Use Each

**Use a hardware volume pedal if:**
- You have no modeler, or the pedal has to work across several rigs and signal paths
- You want amp clean-up and your rig has nothing to drive a volume block
- A buffer already comes before it, so the loading costs above don't apply

**Use an expression pedal for volume if:**
- You're on a modeler; you can put the volume block exactly where you want it in the chain
- You want clean-up without pickup loading (block before the amp) or swells that keep tails (block after the amp)
- You want the same pedal to do volume in one preset and wah or reverb mix in another
- You're running direct to the PA or in-ears and want level control that doesn't change your top end

For technique, see [The Volume Pedal as a Dynamics Control](/blog/volume-pedal-dynamics-control) and [Volume Swell Technique](/blog/volume-swell-technique).

---

## The Bottom Line

On a modeler, an expression pedal driving a volume block does everything a passive volume pedal does, plus it can sit after the amp to keep your tails, and it doesn't load your pickups. The passive pedal is still the right tool without a modeler, and it's fine in front of one if a buffer comes first. If you've been running a passive pedal straight from the guitar into the modeler and your top end feels a little shut, try the same move with an expression pedal and a volume block before the amp. You'll get the same clean-up and keep the treble.
