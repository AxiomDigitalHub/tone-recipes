You learn a song you love. You get about forty seconds in and realise the thing that makes it sound like *that* is two guitars doing two different jobs at once, and there is one of you.

This is the most common arrangement problem there is, and the internet mostly answers the opposite question. Search it and you get page after page of advice for bands with two guitarists covering a one-guitar song — who plays what, how to stay out of each other's way. Useful. Not this.

So: one player, two written parts. Here is how to decide.

## Step One: Work Out What Each Part Is Doing

Before you choose, name the job. Almost every two-guitar arrangement splits along the same line.

| | The foundation part | The identity part |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Holds the chords and the rhythmic pulse | Carries the figure people recognise |
| When it plays | Nearly continuously | In specific windows |
| If you drop it | The song loses its floor and the bass has to carry harmony alone | The song is still the song, but blander |
| Usual location | Low and mid strings, full voicings | Top strings, single notes or dyads |
| Who notices | The band, immediately | The listener, immediately |

That last row is the trap. The identity part is the one *you* notice missing, because it is the reason you learned the song. The foundation part is the one the room notices missing, because without it the arrangement has a hole where its harmony used to be.

Given a forced choice, play the foundation. The song survives a missing hook. It does not survive a missing floor.

## Step Two: Stop Forcing the Choice

Here is the part that actually solves this, and it took me embarrassingly long to see.

Go back to the song and mark every bar where the identity part is playing. Not where it exists in your memory — where it actually sounds. In most two-guitar songs, the signature figure occupies the intro, the turnaround into the chorus, and maybe a lift in the last chorus. Call it twenty percent of the runtime.

The other eighty percent, that guitarist is playing something supportive, or something doubling the first part, or nothing at all.

Which means you are not choosing between two full parts. You are playing one full part and inserting a figure at four or five specific moments. That is a completely different practice problem, and a much smaller one.

I expected the combined part to be roughly twice the work. It was not. It was the foundation part plus about eight bars, and the eight bars were the fun ones. What made it hard was not the notes — it was that the foundation had to be automatic enough to survive my attention leaving it, and that is a separate thing you fix by learning the foundation on its own first.

## Step Three: Build the Third Part

Sometimes the two parts genuinely overlap and you need both at once. That is where register does the work.

The ear separates sources by pitch range far more readily than by anything else. Two things happening in the same octave read as one busy thing. Two things two octaves apart read as two players, even when one hand is producing both.

The move is simple. Root or fifth on the low strings. Figure on the top two. Leave the middle alone.

| Song asks for | One-guitar version |
|---|---|
| Full strummed chords plus a high melodic figure | Bass note on the low E or A, figure on the B and high E, nothing in between |
| Two interlocking riffs in the same register | Play the rhythmically defining one, move the other up an octave and reduce it to its first and last note |
| Chords plus an arpeggiated pattern | Let the arpeggio imply the chord and drop the strumming entirely |
| Palm-muted low riff plus open high chords | Alternate rather than combine, one per bar or one per phrase |

The middle-register gap is doing more than you would think. It is the same principle as two players deliberately splitting the range so they stop colliding, which is covered from the two-player side in [two guitarists, one frequency range](/blog/two-guitar-arrangement-register-split).

If the two registers need to sound at the same instant, hybrid picking is the technique that makes it playable — thumb or pick on the low string, fingers on the top two. It is worth the couple of weeks it takes to get comfortable, and [the starting exercises](/blog/how-to-start-hybrid-picking) are not difficult.

## What a Looper Can and Cannot Do

The obvious answer is to loop one part and play the other. Sometimes it is right. Often it is not, and the failure is predictable.

A loop is one idea, at one volume, at one tempo, forever. That is a perfect match for a song built on a static repeating bed and a bad match for a song that develops.

**Works:** an unchanging texture under a moving part. Drone-based material. A chord bed that stays identical from verse to chorus. Anything where the second guitar is atmosphere rather than arrangement.

**Does not work:** a part that drops out for the bridge. A part that gets louder into the chorus. Anything with a ritard, a fermata, or a tempo that breathes. Any song where the two guitars trade roles halfway through.

Test it honestly before you commit an evening to it: play the song and ask whether the second part is *identical* in the verse and the chorus. If it is not, the looper is going to fight the arrangement, and you will spend more effort managing the loop than you saved by having it.

For the songs where it does fit, treating the looper as a compositional device rather than a playback machine changes what you get out of it, and that is the subject of [the looper as a compositional tool](/blog/looper-pedal-composition).

## The Answer Nobody Offers: Give the Part Away

This is the option that never appears in the arrangement advice, and it is frequently the best one.

If there is a keyboard in the room, the sustained chordal part is easier on keys than on guitar, and it frees you to play the figure that actually needs a guitar. If the missing part is mostly root movement, the bass can imply it with a slightly busier line. If the second guitar was doubling a vocal melody, the vocalist is already covering it.

You are not obligated to reproduce a recording with the instruments you happen to have. A song arranged for the players in the room usually sounds better than a song arranged for the players on the record and executed at seventy percent.

The version of this I resisted longest was letting a part simply not exist. Some doubled parts are there for thickness on a record and add nothing in a room with real air in it. Cutting one is not a compromise. It is an arrangement decision, and it is often the right one.

## If You Are Recording Instead

Different problem, better tools. This is the one context where you genuinely get both parts, because you can play them separately and stack them.

Do that rather than writing a combined part. A combined part is a compromise built for real-time constraints that do not apply when you can record twice. Track the foundation, track the figure, and let them be two performances — which also gets you the natural timing and tone differences between takes that make two tracks read as two players instead of one player copied. There is a fast approach to this in [double-tracking on a time budget](/blog/double-tracking-on-a-time-budget).

The one thing to watch: two guitars you recorded yourself will sit in exactly the same frequency range, because it is the same guitar into the same rig. Change something. Different pickup, capo the second part up five frets, roll the tone back on one. Any of those buys you separation that panning alone will not.

## Where to Start Tonight

Take the song that prompted this. Play the recording and write down the bar numbers where the second guitar's signature figure actually sounds. That list is shorter than you think, and it is the whole map.

Learn the foundation part until you can hold a conversation over it. Then add the figure at the first entry point only. Then the second. If you have twenty minutes at a time, this is a good use of them — one entry point per session, and it compounds faster than trying to play the combined part badly from the start. (The [twenty-minute session guide](/blog/20-minute-practice-session) has more on structuring practice that gets interrupted.)

And if you get to the end and the combined part still does not feel like the record, check whether the record is two guitars or two guitars plus a keyboard plus a doubled vocal. Quite often it is, and you have been trying to play four things with six strings.
