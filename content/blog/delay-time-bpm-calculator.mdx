> **The formula:**
> - **Quarter note delay:** 60,000 ÷ BPM = milliseconds
> - **Dotted eighth note delay:** 60,000 ÷ BPM × 0.75 = milliseconds
> - **Eighth note delay:** 60,000 ÷ BPM × 0.5 = milliseconds
> - **Sixteenth note delay:** 60,000 ÷ BPM × 0.25 = milliseconds

To calculate delay time by BPM, divide 60,000 by the tempo and you have one beat in milliseconds; everything else is a fraction of that. Tap tempo saves you the math in 4/4, but not in 6/8 (see below), and not when you're building presets before rehearsal. The tables below give you the values.

## Why the Dotted Eighth Matters More Than the Quarter Note

The dotted eighth note delay is the defining delay sound of modern worship and ambient guitar, the pattern behind U2's "Where the Streets Have No Name" and most of Hillsong United's ambient builds.

Why does it work? A dotted eighth is three sixteenth notes. Play a note on the beat, and the first repeat arrives on the last sixteenth before the next beat (the "a" if you count "1 e and a"). The second repeat lands on the "and" of the next beat, the third on the following "e". Now play straight eighths: your picked notes sit on the beats and the "ands", and the repeats fill the "e" and "a" between them. You hear continuous sixteenths while only playing half of them. That's the whole trick of the Edge's part: a moving texture that sounds twice as busy as the hands.

Compare this to a quarter note delay, where the repeat falls exactly on the next beat you'd normally play. Quarter note delay creates parallel doubling: the same phrase happening twice, which can support a part but doesn't add any subdivisions.

For a song at 76 BPM:
- **Quarter note delay:** 789 ms
- **Dotted eighth delay:** 592 ms

I used to describe the dotted-eighth repeat as landing "halfway between beats," and it's a common way to explain it. Count it against a click and it's wrong: 592 ms is three-quarters of the way to the next beat, one sixteenth short of it. That matters when you check a delay by ear. A repeat that sits exactly halfway, on the "and," is a straight eighth, which means your time is too short.

Once you hear it correctly synced, an out-of-tempo delay sounds genuinely wrong, like the guitar is a hair behind the band. For more ways to set it by ear, see our guide to [setting a dotted eighth without tap tempo](/blog/dotted-eighth-delay-no-tap-tempo).

## When the Song Is in 6/8

The formulas above assume the BPM counts quarter notes. In 6/8 there are six eighth notes per bar, grouped into two beats of three, and the big beat you sway to is a dotted quarter. Charts and click apps don't agree on what to count. MultiTracks generally lists 6/8 songs by eighth-note pairs, so a song you feel at about 48 beats per minute shows up as 72. If you plug that 72 into a 4/4 dotted-eighth formula, you get 625 ms, which is one and a half eighth notes and lands between the notes of the triplet grouping every time.

Use these instead when the listed BPM counts eighth-note pairs:

| Listed BPM (6/8) | Eighth note, 30,000 ÷ BPM | Felt beat (dotted quarter), 90,000 ÷ BPM | Avoid: "dotted eighth", 45,000 ÷ BPM |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 | 500 | 1500 | 750 |
| 66 | 455 | 1364 | 682 |
| 68 | 441 | 1324 | 662 |
| 72 | 417 | 1250 | 625 |
| 76 | 395 | 1184 | 592 |
| 78 | 385 | 1154 | 577 |
| 84 | 357 | 1071 | 536 |

If your chart gives the tempo as the felt beat instead (a "52" where MultiTracks says 78), the eighth note is 20,000 ÷ BPM. Either way, the eighth-note delay is the one to reach for on strummed and arpeggiated parts: every repeat lands where the next note of the pattern would anyway, so it thickens the part without adding a new rhythm. The one-beat delay is the audible one, a single echo per sway under a held chord.

Tap tempo needs the same care. A Helix treats each tap as a quarter note, so tapping the felt beat of a 6/8 song makes every note value one and a half times too long; after tapping, 1/8 triplet gives you the real eighth and 1/4 gives you the beat. Our [Gratitude build](/blog/gratitude-brandon-lake-guitar-tone-helix) walks through that on a real song at 78 BPM.

## Using a Preset Delay Time When Tap Tempo Isn't Available

Most gigging worship and band situations use tap tempo, either directly on the delay pedal or through a MIDI controller. The formula matters most in three situations:

**1. Preset-based playing:** If you're programming presets for specific songs (a set list locked to specific tempos), calculate the value and enter it directly. The Strymon Timeline, Boss DD-500, and most looper-delay pedals allow entering delay time by millisecond. Use the table.

**2. Clock-based signal chain:** Some setups receive MIDI clock from a DAW or playback rig. MIDI clock sends 24 pulses per quarter note, so the delay follows the click, but knowing the expected millisecond value lets you check the sync is working. On a 6/8 song, check what the click is counting before you trust it. Our guide to [syncing a Helix to Playback or Ableton](/blog/helix-midi-clock-playback-click-worship) covers the setup and what happens between songs.

**3. Older pedals without tap tempo:** The Boss DD-3, original Boss DM-2, and many vintage-style analog delays don't have tap tempo. For these, calculate the time, set it before the song starts, and adjust during soundcheck. A 5ms error at 120 BPM is barely perceptible; a 30ms error is noticeably off.

## Setting Feedback and Mix for Tempo-Synced Delay

Getting the time right is step one. Getting the feedback and mix right determines whether the repeats sit behind the part like a second guitarist a few feet back, or pile up into a wash that blurs the strumming.

**Feedback (number of repeats):** For dotted eighth rhythmic delay, 2–3 audible repeats is the working range. More than 4 audible repeats and the rhythmic texture starts to stack on itself and muddy the time feel. Less than 2 and the effect loses its momentum. Start at 3 audible repeats and adjust.

**Mix (wet/dry ratio):** The exact setting depends on your role in the mix. As a lead or featured player, 20–30% wet is appropriate; the repeats are audible and contribute to the texture but the dry signal is clearly dominant. For atmospheric or pad-style playing where you're explicitly creating texture rather than a lead melody, 30–50% wet opens the delay up significantly.

One thing I've learned playing in large church services: the mix setting that sounds right through my in-ears is almost always too high for what the congregation hears through the PA. The room adds its own reflection and delay-like smearing to the signal. Dial in a mix level that sounds slightly dry to you in your ears. The room will fill it back in.

## Dotted Eighth vs. Triplet Delay

"Triplet delay" means two different settings, and pedals don't always say which one they use. An **eighth-note triplet** is a third of a beat (20,000 ÷ BPM). A **quarter-note triplet** is two-thirds of a beat (40,000 ÷ BPM), which is the one that sounds like a lazy, rolling echo.

| BPM | Half note (ms) | Quarter-note triplet (ms) | Eighth-note triplet (ms) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 | 2000 | 667 | 333 |
| 72 | 1667 | 556 | 278 |
| 80 | 1500 | 500 | 250 |
| 90 | 1333 | 444 | 222 |
| 100 | 1200 | 400 | 200 |
| 120 | 1000 | 333 | 167 |
| 140 | 857 | 286 | 143 |

At 120 BPM the eighth-note triplet is 167 ms, almost a slapback, while the quarter-note triplet at 333 ms is a clear echo that pulls against straight eighths. Both suit blues, country and shuffles more than the ambient textures the dotted eighth creates. For straight-ahead rock, a quarter note or half note keeps the repeats on the beat. The delay value is a rhythmic choice as much as a technical one.
