> **The essential distinction:** Preamp distortion comes from the amp's gain stages before the output transformer. Power tube saturation comes from the output tubes themselves (6L6s, EL34s, 6V6s) and the transformer they drive, running at the limits of their design. Each stage produces a different harmonic character, a different dynamic response, and a different feel under your fingers.

| | Preamp distortion | Power tube saturation |
|---|---|---|
| Where it happens | Gain stages before the output transformer | Output tubes and output transformer under high load |
| How to get it | Turn up the gain or preamp volume control | Turn up the master volume (or use a single-volume amp at a high setting) |
| Harmonic character | A lightly driven stage is mostly second harmonic; several stages clipping hard fill in dense high-order products — the fizz | Push-pull output cancels the tubes' even harmonics; the round, heavy part comes from transformer saturation, sag, and the speaker |
| Dynamic response | Compresses earlier, less dynamic range | More dynamic; responds more to pick attack |
| Sag | Absent or minimal | Present (power supply briefly sags on loud attacks) |
| Classic example | JCM800 preamp cranked, master at 3 | Plexi at volume 7–8, no master volume |

## The Amp as Two Separate Distortion Machines

A tube amp is not a single gain stage. It's a series of amplification stages: the preamp section (where gain controls and tone shaping happen) feeds a power section (where output tubes amplify the signal to speaker-driving power levels). Both sections can distort. They distort differently.

Most players understand preamp distortion intuitively because preamp-stage distortion is what happens when you turn the gain knob up. It's fast, it's responsive to gain control, and it's the dominant distortion character in most modern amp designs — the JCM800, the Marshall JVM, the Peavey 5150, the Mesa Boogie Dual Rectifier. These amps have substantial preamp gain. The gain control is doing most of the work.

Power tube saturation is what happens when the output tubes themselves are pushed past their comfortable operating range. This requires volume (actual volume, speaker-moving volume) because the output tubes are driving the output transformer and speakers, and they only saturate when they're being asked to produce more than they comfortably can.

The two things most players know about this but rarely see explained clearly:

1. **Master volume controls on modern amps can separate the two.** A high-gain preamp with a master volume at 2 gives you preamp distortion without power tube saturation. The same preamp at a moderate gain setting with master volume at 8 gives you some power tube saturation without maximum preamp distortion.

2. **Old Plexis and other single-volume amps have no way to separate the two.** There's one volume control. It raises both the preamp signal level and the output power level simultaneously. Getting the power tubes to saturate means making the amp loud. That's the defining constraint of the pre-master-volume era of amplifier design, and also why those amps sound the way they sound.

## The Harmonic Story: Why They Sound Different

Here's the part that explains the perceptual difference, and it's the part most often told backwards.

Vacuum tubes distort through a specific mechanism: when the signal exceeds the tube's linear operating region, the tube clips. It can't amplify any higher, so the peaks of the waveform get flattened. The shape of that clipping determines the harmonic content of the distortion.

The shorthand you'll read everywhere is "preamp distortion is odd-order, power tube distortion is even-order." For most guitar amps, that's close to backwards.

**Preamp stages** in guitar amps are almost always single-ended triode gain stages — one 12AX7 half handling the whole waveform with no mirror-image partner. Push a single-ended stage and it clips asymmetrically: the positive and negative half-cycles don't hit their limits the same way. That asymmetry is exactly what generates even-order harmonics, and a lightly overdriven triode stage is dominated by the second harmonic, an octave above the note. Stack three or four of those stages into hard clipping, as a high-gain preamp does, and the spectrum fills with dense high-order products of both kinds. That's the fizzy, edgy, filed-down quality of high-gain preamp distortion as it's pushed further.

**Power stages** in most guitar amps (Marshall Plexi, JCM800, Bassman, Twin Reverb, Mesa, AC30) are *push-pull*. Two output tubes, or two pairs, work in opposition: one handles the positive half-cycle, the other the negative. A balanced push-pull stage *cancels* even-order harmonics by topology, leaving odd-order products behind. So "power tubes = even-order" is only accurate for *single-ended Class A* amps (Fender tweed Champ, Vox AC4, some old Princeton circuits). On a Plexi or a Bassman, the output stage is dominantly odd-order when it saturates.

So where does the round, heavy quality everyone calls warmth in a cranked Plexi actually come from? Not from the tubes' even harmonics. It comes from a stack of effects the push-pull arithmetic doesn't cancel:

- **Output-transformer saturation.** The transformer's magnetic core has its own clipping behavior, and transformer saturation produces strong even-order content, especially at low frequencies.
- **Sag and power-supply compression** (see next section). The tubes run on a moving voltage rail, which softens peaks and bloom-compresses notes in a way no pedal replicates.
- **Crossover distortion** at the zero crossing in Class AB operation, which adds its own spectral signature, most noticeably on quiet and decaying notes.
- **Speaker and cab interaction.** A guitar speaker's impedance curve fights back against the amp's output impedance, and that interaction shapes transient response and harmonic content in ways the amp alone can't.

The practical implication: if you want the fat breakup that sounds like it's coming from *inside the amp* — not like a pedal, not like processed gain, but like the whole amplifier is singing — you're hearing the combined effect of transformer saturation, sag, speaker interaction and some tube nonlinearity. Getting that through a small bedroom amp or a modeler at low volume requires a power attenuator (preserves most of it), a lower-wattage amp (same effects at more manageable volumes), or a good power-stage model with its Master turned up (see below).

## The Sag Factor

Power tube saturation comes with a companion effect: sag. When the output tubes draw more current from the power supply than usual (on loud, sustained notes or hard attack peaks), the power supply voltage briefly drops. The tubes run at lower voltage for a moment, then the voltage recovers.

This produces a specific dynamic characteristic: loud attacks bloom and soften slightly as the supply sags, then recover as voltage comes back. It's a natural compression that the player feels more than consciously hears. The guitar's attack feels connected to something physical. Notes sustain in a particular way.

No preamp distortion circuit produces sag. Some attenuators reduce it. Some amp designs (particularly cathode-biased designs like tweed Fenders and the Vox AC30) have more sag than fixed-bias designs (like the Plexi Marshall). The full treatment is in [what amp sag is and why it changes feel](/blog/what-is-amp-sag).

When players say a modeler "feels stiff," sag behavior is often what they're actually describing. The notes don't have that subtle give.

## How to Get Power Tube Saturation in Practice

### Real Tube Amps

For a single-volume amp (Plexi, tweed Deluxe, AC30): the only way is volume. These amps are the gold standard for power tube saturation, and the trade-off is that getting the saturation means making the amp loud. At 100 watts, a Plexi pushing its output tubes means stage volume that clears a 2,000-seat venue. A 15-watt tweed Deluxe can be pushed into power tube saturation at more manageable volumes.

For a master-volume amp: leave the preamp gain lower than usual (so you're not front-loading the system with preamp distortion) and raise the master volume until the amp starts to breathe. On a JCM800 2203 or 2204, that means backing off the preamp volume and letting the master carry more of the load. The [guide to amp gain and volume controls](/blog/amp-gain-volume-master-controls) walks through that interaction stage by stage.

A power attenuator (the Two-Notes Torpedo Reload, the Weber Mass, or the Fryette Power Station) lets you push a large amp's output stage to saturation and then attenuate the signal before the speaker cabinet. The tubes work at full load; the volume at the speaker comes down. The sag and transformer character survive largely intact. This is the most practical way to get real power tube saturation at reasonable volumes.

### Modelers

On a Helix, the Master parameter in the amp block is the power-amp drive control, not an output level, and Helix Help's reference is plain that the lower it sits, the less every other power-amp parameter does. That's the first thing to know: a preset with Drive at 7 and Master at 3 has almost no modeled power tube saturation in it, whatever Sag and Bias say. Turn Master up and bring Drive down to compensate, and three parameters come alive — Sag (the supply droop), Bias (where the virtual output tubes idle, from colder Class AB up to Class A at maximum), and Bias X (how far that idle point shifts when you hit the power amp hard). [Bias and Bias X](/blog/modeler-bias-and-bias-x-what-they-do) covers those two in detail.

Fractal's equivalents are Supply Sag, Power Tube Bias and Master Bias Excursion, with defaults tuned per model. On a Kemper, Power Sagging covers the supply side, and Kemper's manual suggests raising Tube Shape toward 9 for a power-tube style of distortion. The Quad Cortex has a Master control; its manual doesn't document power-stage parameters beyond that, and a capture has the power stage baked in at whatever level the amp was captured.

None of these replications are perfect. The best modeler power-stage emulations are close enough that the difference is debatable at mix volumes. At louder volumes through a physical cab, where the speaker's response to transients becomes another variable, the real thing still produces a tactile response that modeling hasn't fully replicated.

## Which Amps Are Famous for Power Tube Saturation?

| Amp | Output tubes | Topology | Saturation character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Marshall 1959 Plexi | EL34s | Class AB push-pull | Aggressive, firm sag, mid-forward. Odd-order dominant from the tubes; the transformer and sag supply the roundness. |
| Fender Tweed Deluxe | 6V6s | Class AB push-pull (cathode-biased) | Saggy and compressed; the note swells after the pick instead of arriving all at once. The softest breakup. |
| Vox AC30 | EL84s | Class A/AB push-pull (cathode-biased) | Glassy, compressed, a chiming bloom. Closer-to-Class-A operation gives it a different harmonic signature than an EL34 Marshall. |
| Fender Bassman (5F6-A) | 6L6s | Class AB push-pull | Fat, round, less compressed than a 6V6 tweed. More headroom, more transformer work at volume. |
| Matchless DC-30 | EL84s | Class A/AB push-pull (cathode-biased) | AC30-derived, more refined, extended headroom. |
| Fender Tweed Champ | 6V6 (single tube) | **Single-ended Class A** | The classic example of genuine even-order tube saturation. One tube, no push-pull cancellation. Fat, round, and thick with second harmonic. |

Each output tube type has its own saturation character. EL84s in the AC30 break up earlier and with more compression than EL34s in a Marshall. 6V6s in a small Fender produce a sweeter, rounder saturation than EL34s. These aren't marketing differences; they're consistent properties of the specific tube designs, and our [6V6 vs. 6L6 vs. EL34](/blog/6v6-vs-6l6-vs-el34-power-tubes) comparison goes tube by tube.
