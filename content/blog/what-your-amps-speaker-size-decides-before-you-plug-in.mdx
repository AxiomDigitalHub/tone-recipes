Speaker size gets discussed almost entirely in adjectives. Twelves are described as fuller, tens as tighter and punchier, eights as boxy, and the whole conversation runs on comparisons nobody has to defend with a number.

There is a dataset that settles most of it. Jensen's Vintage Alnico P-series is published in three diameters with the magnet, the voice coil and the power rating held constant. Same 200 g Alnico magnet. Same 25 mm voice coil. Same 25 W rating. The only thing that changes is the cone.

## The Controlled Comparison

Jensen Vintage Alnico, 8-ohm versions, from the manufacturer's own specification sheets:

| Model | Frame diameter | Rated power | Sensitivity (1 W, 1 m) | Resonance (Fs) | Voice coil | Magnet |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P8R | 8 in / 207 mm | 25 W | 91.8 dB | 135 Hz | 25 mm | Alnico, 200 g |
| P10R | 10 in / 259 mm | 25 W | 94.2 dB | 97 Hz | 25 mm | Alnico, 200 g |
| P12R | 12 in / 307 mm | 25 W | 93.7 dB | 80 Hz | 25 mm | Alnico, 200 g |

Resonance falls 135 to 97 to 80 Hz as the cone grows. That is the size trade, stated by the maker, with every other variable pinned.

Now the number that reframes it. **A standard-tuned open low E is 82.41 Hz.**

The 8-inch resonates 52 Hz above it. The 10-inch resonates 15 Hz above it. The 12-inch resonates 2 Hz below it. On an 8 or a 10, the lowest note on your guitar has its fundamental sitting underneath the speaker's own resonant peak, in the region where output is already falling away. On a 12 it is right at the peak.

That is the mechanism behind "tens are tighter." There is less energy down where the fundamental lives, so what you hear is weighted toward the second harmonic at 164.8 Hz and up. Less fundamental reads as definition, because definition is largely the absence of competing low-frequency energy.

## The Part I Had Backwards

I expected sensitivity to track diameter. More cone area for the same motor should move more air for the same watt, and larger drivers are generally more efficient in the ranges they cover.

It does not hold here. The P10R is published at 94.2 dB and the P12R at 93.7 dB — the 10-inch is half a decibel louder than the 12-inch, from the same maker, with the same magnet and the same coil. Only the 8-inch behaves as expected, at 91.8 dB.

Half a decibel is inside most people's tolerance for "the same," and I would not build an argument on it in isolation. What matters is the direction: the efficiency gain from 8 to 10 inches is 2.4 dB, and from 10 to 12 inches it is negative. The larger cone spends its extra area on reaching lower rather than on being louder, because a bigger, heavier cone with the same motor trades sensitivity for extension. The specs make that trade visible, and the folklore version — bigger is louder — does not survive it.

## Cross-Checking Against Celestion

Jensen's set is controlled but it is one maker and one magnet type. Celestion publishes across diameters too, with a frequency range figure Jensen does not provide. These are not matched models, so read the trend rather than the individual rows:

| Model | Diameter | Power | Sensitivity | Published range | Resonance | Voice coil |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Eight 15 | 8 in | 20 W | 95 dB | 100–5000 Hz | 120 Hz | 1 in |
| Ten 30 | 10 in | 30 W | 95 dB | 85–5000 Hz | 93.9 Hz | 1.25 in |
| G10 Greenback | 10 in | 30 W | 95 dB | 95–5500 Hz | 98 Hz | 1.75 in |
| G12M Greenback | 12 in | 25 W | 98 dB | 75–5000 Hz | 75 Hz | 1.75 in |
| G12T-75 | 12 in | 75 W | 97 dB | 80–5000 Hz | 85 Hz | 1.75 in |
| Vintage 30 | 12 in | 60 W | 100 dB | 70–5000 Hz | 75 Hz | 1.75 in |

Resonance moves the same direction: 120 Hz at 8 inches, 93.9 and 98 at 10, 75 to 85 at 12. Two independent makers, two magnet types, same trend.

The Greenback pair is the most useful row because the two share a voice coil diameter and a model family. The 10-inch is published 95–5500 Hz and the 12-inch 75–5000 Hz. The smaller cone starts 20 Hz higher and extends 500 Hz further up. You give up low-frequency reach and you get back high-frequency reach, from the same maker's same design intent.

Note the G12T-75, though. Its 85 Hz resonance is above the open low E, and it is a 12. Diameter is a strong predictor, not a law — a heavier cone built for 75 W of power handling pays for that handling in resonance. The Vintage 30 and the G12M Greenback, both at 75 Hz, bracket it from below.

## The Honesty Problem With These Figures

Three makers, three different definitions.

- **Celestion** publishes "Frequency range" with **no dB tolerance stated**. A range of 75–5000 Hz does not say whether 75 Hz is the −3 dB point, the −6 dB point or the −10 dB point. You cannot convert it into a filter curve, and you should not treat a 5 Hz difference between two Celestion models as meaningful.
- **Eminence** labels the comparable figure a "usable frequency range," which is explicitly a judgement rather than a measurement threshold.
- **Jensen** publishes no range at all for this line, but does publish resonance and the full Thiele-Small parameter set.

Resonance is the physically defined quantity of the three. It is a measurable property of the moving mass and the suspension compliance, it is reported the same way by everyone, and it is the only figure in these tables I would compare across brands without qualification. The range figures are useful inside one maker's catalogue and close to meaningless between them.

This also means the tables above cannot tell you what a cab does below resonance, only where resonance is. For what a mounted speaker in a box actually passes down there, [the low-frequency rolloff question has its own article with the response-curve caveats](/blog/guitar-speaker-low-end-rolloff-what-a-cab-actually-passes).

## Four Tens Against One Twelve

The 4x10 versus 1x12 question comes up constantly and the folk answer — four tens give you the low end of a bigger speaker — is wrong in a specific, checkable way.

Frontal area scales with the square of diameter. From the published frame diameters:

| Configuration | Relative frontal area |
|---|---|
| 1x10 (259 mm) | 0.71 |
| 1x12 (307 mm) | 1.00 |
| 2x12 | 2.00 |
| 4x10 | 2.85 |

So four tens present roughly 2.85 times the frontal area of one twelve. That is real, and it is why a 4x10 moves air in a room in a way a 1x12 does not.

What it does not do is lower the resonance. Resonance is a property of each driver — its moving mass against its suspension compliance — and putting four of them in one box does not change either. Four P10Rs resonate at 97 Hz, exactly where one P10R resonates. The array is louder, handles more power and displaces more air. Its low-frequency corner is in the same place.

Which gives you the honest comparison: a 4x10 is a louder 10-inch cabinet, not a bigger-sounding 12-inch cabinet. If you want the 12-inch bottom end, you need a 12-inch driver. If you want level and air movement with the 10-inch midrange and the 10-inch range floor, the 4x10 is exactly the right tool.

One caveat on that table: frame diameter is not radiating area. The cone's effective piston area is smaller than the frame it sits in, and the ratio differs by cone profile and surround design. The correct parameter is Sd, which Jensen publishes in its Thiele-Small data and which I did not read for this comparison. Treat the area column as proportional rather than absolute.

## Drop Tunings: Diameter Is the Wrong Lever

This is where the whole discussion stops being about cone size.

| Tuning | Lowest open string | Fundamental |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | E2 | 82.41 Hz |
| Eb standard | Eb2 | 77.78 Hz |
| D standard / drop D | D2 | 73.42 Hz |
| Drop C | C2 | 65.41 Hz |
| 7-string standard | B1 | 61.74 Hz |
| 8-string standard | F#1 | 46.25 Hz |

Compare that column against the resonance figures. **Every entry below standard tuning sits beneath the resonance of every speaker in both tables, 12-inch models included.** Drop C at 65.41 Hz is 10 Hz under the lowest resonance here. An 8-string low F# at 46.25 Hz is not within 29 Hz of it.

Which means the fundamental of your lowest string is not what you are hearing. You are hearing the second harmonic and up, with the fundamental attenuated by the driver's own rolloff and by whatever the cabinet does below resonance. This is not a defect to engineer around. It is why drop-tuned rhythm tone is built in the 100–250 Hz region and the 2–4 kHz region, and why a high-pass filter below about 70 Hz costs you nothing audible while removing cone excursion that was producing distortion rather than notes.

So for a drop-tuned rig, diameter is low on the list. Power handling, midrange voicing and cone breakup behaviour all matter more, because all three happen in the band where your actual tone lives. Pick the speaker for its midrange, then handle the bottom with [gain staging and filtering](/blog/gain-staging-drop-tunings).

## What This Means for Impulse Responses

The practical consequence for a direct rig is that a 10-inch impulse response carries the 10-inch range floor with it, and most high-pass advice does not account for that.

The standard recommendation is to high-pass a guitar IR somewhere around 80 to 100 Hz. On a 12-inch capture with a 75 Hz resonance, that filter is removing content the speaker genuinely produced. On a 10-inch capture with a 97 Hz resonance, the same filter is cutting into territory the driver had already attenuated, and you are applying the cut twice. The result is a thinner tone than the same filter produces on a 12-inch IR, and the usual response is to blame the IR.

Check the capture's driver before setting the filter. If the IR is named for a 4x10 or a 1x10, start the high-pass higher — somewhere in the 100 to 120 Hz region — and listen for whether the body comes back rather than assuming the published advice transfers. [The IR fundamentals guide](/blog/impulse-response-ir-guide) covers what a capture does and does not contain.

## Summary

| If you want | Pick | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Fundamental on a standard-tuned low E | 12 inch | Resonance at or below 82.41 Hz |
| Articulation and less low-frequency competition | 10 inch | Resonance 15 Hz above the low E |
| Maximum level from the 10-inch voicing | 4x10 | 2.85× the frontal area of a 1x12, same range floor |
| Low-end reach in a drop tuning | Neither | No guitar speaker reproduces those fundamentals |
| Published figures you can compare across brands | Resonance | The only one defined the same way by everyone |

The adjectives were not wrong. Tens are tighter and twelves are fuller. What the spec sheets add is the reason, the size of the effect, and the point at which the reason stops applying — which is the moment you tune below E, where every driver in the catalogue is doing the same thing.
