> **Quick answer:** The knobs carry over; the back half of the amp does not. Set both amps to the same numbers below about Volume 5 and you are working with the same preamp and tone stack — the Tone Master was built to copy that part, and it is the part the faceplate controls. Past that, the tube amp's sound depends on two 6V6s, a 5AR4 rectifier, a real spring tank and a ceramic Jensen, and the Tone Master replaces all four. The Tone Master's power selector is the reason to buy one; the tube amp's output stage is the reason not to.

| | '65 Deluxe Reverb reissue (tube) | Tone Master Deluxe Reverb |
|---|---|---|
| Rated power | 22 W into 8 Ω | 100 W into 8 Ω, rated "22 W tube equivalent" |
| Output stage | 2 × 6V6, 5AR4 tube rectifier | Digital power amp |
| Power selector | None | 22 / 12 / 5 / 1 / 0.5 / 0.2 W |
| Speaker | Jensen C-12K, ceramic magnet | Jensen N-12K, neodymium (black); Celestion Neo Creamback (Blonde) |
| Reverb | Spring tank, 12AT7 driver | Digital |
| Direct output | None | Balanced XLR line out with IR cab simulation, level control, ground switch |
| Effects loop | None | None |
| Weight | about 42 lb | about 23 lb |

*Specifications from Fender's product listings as carried by its dealers. Street prices on both have moved more than once since the Tone Master line arrived — check current pricing rather than trusting any figure printed here, including ours.*

## What the Faceplate Actually Controls

The blackface Deluxe Reverb panel — Volume, Treble and Bass on the Normal channel; Volume, Treble, Bass, Reverb, Speed and Intensity on the Vibrato channel — sits in front of the preamp and the tone stack, and almost nothing else. That is worth saying out loud because it explains the whole comparison. When you match knob positions across the two amps, you are matching the part of the circuit the Tone Master was designed to reproduce, and the part a model reproduces most convincingly, because a preamp running clean is a well-behaved thing to model.

The tone stack in particular is a fixed network of resistors and capacitors, and the knobs move it through a known range. At Volume 3 or 4 with Treble at 6 and Bass at 4, both amps are doing the same arithmetic on your signal. That is why reports of the two being hard to tell apart at low volume are believable without anyone having to take them on faith: at that setting, the parts of the tube amp the Tone Master does not have are barely working.

The [Deluxe Reverb settings guide](/blog/fender-deluxe-reverb-settings) has the full song-by-song settings, and the [Normal vs. Vibrato channel post](/blog/fender-normal-vs-vibrato-channel) explains why the Vibrato channel's extra gain stage makes it the louder, brighter one on both amps.

## Where the Numbers Stop Meaning the Same Thing

Turn the tube amp's Volume past 5 and three things start happening that have nothing to do with the faceplate.

**The 6V6s start to compress.** A Deluxe Reverb's output stage is small — two 6V6s, 22 watts — and it runs out of headroom early, which is most of why the amp breaks up at a volume a club can still stand. The breakup arrives gradually and leans on how hard you pick. We covered how to tell that apart from preamp grind in [preamp vs. power-amp distortion](/blog/preamp-vs-power-amp-distortion-how-to-hear), and the [6V6 vs. 6L6 vs. EL34 post](/blog/6v6-vs-6l6-vs-el34-power-tubes) explains why a 6V6 gets there sooner than its bigger cousins.

**The rectifier sags.** The 5AR4 is a tube rectifier, so the supply voltage dips when you hit a big chord and recovers when you let go. That dip is the slight lag-and-bloom players describe at Volume 6 and up. [Tube vs. solid-state rectifier sag](/blog/tube-vs-solid-state-rectifier-vintage-fender-sag) has the mechanism.

**The speaker starts doing real work.** At low volume a speaker is a fairly obedient transducer. Near the tube amp's limit it is being driven hard enough that its own compression and breakup join in.

The Tone Master replaces all three with a model and a 100-watt digital power amp, and Fender rates it as 22 watts tube-equivalent so that Volume 7 on its panel means roughly what Volume 7 means on the original. That is the right engineering goal. But once you are past the point where the tube amp's output stage is contributing, the two amps are no longer running the same circuit with the same numbers on it — one is running the circuit, the other is running a description of it. Whether you can hear the difference at Volume 7 in your room is something only your room can answer; that the difference starts *there*, rather than at Volume 3, follows from where the parts are.

## The Speaker Nobody Matched

Here is the part that surprises people who read the spec sheets side by side. I expected the reissue and its digital sibling to share a speaker, the way they share a faceplate — and plenty of comparison write-ups, including an earlier version of this one, assumed they did. They do not. The '65 reissue ships with a **Jensen C-12K**, a ceramic-magnet speaker. The black Tone Master ships with a **Jensen N-12K**, a neodymium one, and the Blonde Tone Master with a **Celestion Neo Creamback**. Fender's choice of a neodymium speaker is no mystery — the whole point of the Tone Master is that it weighs about 23 pounds against the tube amp's 42, and a neodymium magnet is a large part of how you take weight out of a combo.

The consequence for a "same settings" test is direct. Two amps with different speakers are not an amp comparison; they are an amp-and-speaker comparison, and the speaker is the single biggest filter in the chain. What a different magnet does and does not change, by the manufacturers' own figures, is the subject of [alnico vs. ceramic, same cone](/blog/alnico-vs-ceramic-speaker-same-cone-published-specs). The short version is that the magnet changes sensitivity and weight more reliably than it changes anything else you can put a number on.

And neither amp's speaker is the one a 1960s original shipped with. A blackface Deluxe Reverb from the period might have left the factory with a Jensen or an Oxford, depending on what Fender's suppliers delivered that month — [Oxford or Jensen](/blog/oxford-vs-jensen-blackface-fender-speakers) covers how to tell which one you have and why it varied. If you are chasing the sound of a particular old record, the speaker on it was not a C-12K and not an N-12K.

## The Power Selector Changes What the Volume Knob Means

This is the Tone Master's real argument, and it is practical rather than tonal.

On the tube Deluxe Reverb, one knob decides two things at once: how loud the amp is and how hard the output stage is working. You cannot have Volume 7's breakup without Volume 7's level, and Volume 7 on a Deluxe is a lot of level for a living room. An external attenuator can split those apart, at a cost we covered in [power scaling vs. attenuators](/blog/power-scaling-vs-attenuator).

The Tone Master builds the split in. Its power selector runs 22, 12, 5, 1, 0.5 and 0.2 watts, and the Volume knob keeps asking the model for the same thing at every position. So the same settings mean something different depending on the selector:

| What you want | Tube '65 reissue | Tone Master |
|---|---|---|
| Clean practice, quiet room | Vibrato channel, Volume 2–3, Treble 6, Bass 4 | Same knobs, selector at 1 W or 0.5 W |
| Edge of breakup, Strat bridge pickup | Volume 5–6 — loud | Volume 5–6, selector at 1–5 W |
| Full breakup | Volume 7–8 — band volume or an attenuator | Volume 7–8, selector at 0.2–1 W for home, 12–22 W with a drummer |
| Silent recording | Mic or reactive load box required | Mute switch plus XLR line out |

Starting points, not gospel — your guitar's output decides where breakup actually begins. The table's one firm claim is the shape: on the tube amp, the breakup rows cost volume; on the Tone Master, they do not.

## Reverb, Weight and the Back Panel

The tube amp's reverb is a physical spring tank driven by a 12AT7, and a tank has a mechanical character — the slap when you hit a string hard, the way it changes when the amp gets bumped — that varies with how it is mounted and how hard it is struck. The Tone Master's reverb is digital and does the same thing every time. Some players hear that consistency as a fault and some as a fix; it is not the same thing either way.

On the back panel, Fender lists for the Tone Master a power selector, a mute switch, a USB port for firmware, and a balanced XLR line out with impulse-response cabinet simulation, a level control and a ground switch — dealer listings describe the cab simulation as captured with a Shure SM57 and a Sennheiser MD421. The tube reissue has no direct output of any kind. If you record at home, that difference settles the purchase before tone gets a vote.

One caution if you plan to run either amp into an external cabinet: Fender's published feature lists for the Tone Master do not include a speaker output, so check the rear panel of the unit in front of you before building a test around one.

## Which One Should You Buy?

**Buy the Tone Master if** you need the Deluxe's breakup at a level your home or a small room can take, you record without a microphone, or you carry your own amp up stairs. The power selector and the line out are the whole case, and it is a strong one.

**Buy the tube reissue if** the reason you want a Deluxe Reverb is what it does at Volume 7 with the output stage, the rectifier and a real spring tank all working, and you have a room — or an attenuator — that lets you get there.

**Either way, budget for the speaker.** Neither ships with the speaker a 1960s original carried, and on a 1x12 combo the speaker moves the sound more than the gap between the two amps' circuits does at most settings.
