When Gibson launched the Victory Baritone last month, the two humbuckers in it were the part I flagged and could not buy. [I wrote at the time](/news/gibson-victory-baritone-28-inch-elev8-alnico-8) that the interesting thing about a $2,499 baritone was a pair of pickups nobody could put in their own guitar yet.

Now you can. The Gibson Pickup Shop has put the **ELEV8** series on the shelf on its own — three humbuckers, **$139 uncovered** in Double Black or Zebra, **$149** with Double Black bobbins under a nickel cover.

Here is the lineup as Gibson specs it:

- **ELEV8** — the standard, reading about **10k ohms**, available in both traditional Gibson neck and bridge spacings
- **ELEV8 Plus** — an overwound bridge pickup at about **15k ohms**, meant to pair with a standard ELEV8 in the neck
- **ELEV8 Underwound** — a neck pickup built to pair with a standard ELEV8 at the bridge, for what Gibson calls a modern/vintage combination

All three run **Alnico 8 magnets** and **four-conductor wiring**, which means the whole range is ready for coil splitting and series/parallel switching out of the box.

## Seventy-One Years, and This Is the First Alnico 8

Gibson's own framing is the part worth sitting with. Seth Lover's humbucker design was patented-applied-for in 1955 and started showing up under those little decals on production guitars in 1957. In the sixty-nine years since, Gibson has wound its humbuckers around **Alnico 2, 3, 4, and 5**. That is the whole historical palette. The Alnico 2 in a Peter Green–era PAF, the Alnico 5 in a Burstbucker Pro, the Alnico 3 that gives an early-'57 set that loose, slightly soft attack — those magnets and the arguments about them are most of what I sold guitars on for twenty-five years.

Alnico 8 has never been in the list until now.

It is worth being straight about what is and is not new here. Alnico 8 is not a new magnet and it is not exotic — the aftermarket has used it for decades, most famously in Seymour Duncan's Alternative 8, which is named for the magnet grade and has been the go-to hot-bridge option for players who found ceramic too brittle. What is new is Gibson using it, and that matters more than it sounds, because Gibson's pickup catalogue is the reference point most players calibrate against. When the company that has been shipping Alnico 5 bridge humbuckers since the Eisenhower administration puts an 8 in a production pickup, that is a statement about where it thinks the market went.

What an Alnico 8 does, physically: it is a substantially stronger magnet than a 5. More coercivity, more energy in the field, more flux through the coil for the same string movement. Gibson pairs it here with custom-gauge wire and describes the result as **exceptional output with a controlled, tight bass response** — which is the accurate description. Ceramic gets you output too, but it does it with a hardness in the upper mids that a lot of people hear as sterile. Alnico 8 lands in between: the output of a hot ceramic set with more of the compression and midrange body of an alnico.

Gibson is aiming it squarely at drop tunings, palm-muted riffing, and dense mixes. That is a fair aim. It is also the exact application where the magnet's strength stops being free.

## The 15k on the Plus Is Not an Output Spec

Before anybody reads "15k ohms" and assumes the Plus is roughly twice as loud as a PAF at 8k, stop.

DC resistance tells you how much wire is on the bobbin. It does not tell you how loud the pickup is, and it barely tells you how it sounds — our [breakdown of what DC resistance actually measures](/blog/pickup-dc-resistance-what-it-actually-measures) goes through this properly, but the short version is that a pickup's output is a product of the winding **and** the magnet, and the magnet is doing a lot of the work here.

Which means the ELEV8 Plus at 15k with an Alnico 8 is meaningfully hotter than a 15k pickup with an Alnico 5 would be, and the standard ELEV8 at 10k — a figure that on paper sounds like a mildly warm vintage-output humbucker — is not going to behave like one. A 10k Alnico 8 is hotter and tighter than a 10k Alnico 5, and if you order the standard expecting a PAF-plus, you will be surprised in the first four bars.

I say this because I watched people buy pickups by ohm readings for twenty-five years and I could never talk most of them out of it.

## The Two Settings You Will Have to Move

Here is the F&K half of this, because a pickup swap is a gain-staging event and your presets do not know it happened.

**One: pull the amp block's Drive back before you judge the pickup.**

Your presets were dialed with whatever came out of your guitar before. Drop a hotter pickup in and every gain stage downstream inherits it — the input, the drive block, the amp's preamp. The tone you hear on the first chord is not the pickup, it is the pickup plus a preset that is now over-driven, and the symptoms are exactly what people then blame on the magnet: mushy low end, compressed feel, chords that lose definition.

Concretely, going from a standard Alnico 5 bridge humbucker to an ELEV8 Plus, I would start by **pulling the Helix amp block's Drive down by roughly 1 to 1.5 on the 0–10 scale** and leaving everything else alone. If you run a Tube Screamer or Timmy-style block in front, drop its Drive too and push its Level to compensate — you want the same volume arriving at the amp block, not the same knob positions. Then re-listen. Half of what people dislike about hot pickups is a preset nobody rebalanced.

The same logic applies harder in drop tunings, which is what these are built for. Our [gain staging for drop tunings guide](/blog/gain-staging-drop-tunings) and Viktor's [drop-tuned high-gain walkthrough](/blog/dialing-in-drop-tuned-high-gain) both land on the same rule: a lower tuning plus a hotter pickup means *less* preamp gain, not more. The tight bass Gibson is advertising only stays tight if the amp block isn't smearing it.

For amp model choices with a hot alnico bridge, the [Helix amp model cheat sheet](/blog/helix-amp-model-cheat-sheet) is the map; my own instinct is that an Alnico 8 does its best work in front of a **Brit 2204 or a Placater Dirty** rather than the highest-gain models, because the pickup is already supplying the aggression and the model only needs to supply the cabinet-and-power-amp half.

**Two: lower the pickup. Then lower it again.**

This is the one that will actually bite people, and it is the one the spec sheet will never mention. A stronger magnet pulls harder on the string. Past a certain proximity, that pull changes how the string moves — it stops swinging freely and starts moving in an elliptical path, which costs you harmonics, costs you sustain, and on the wound strings at the neck produces an audible warble as the note decays.

Carl's piece on [magnet pull and pickup height](/blog/pickup-height-magnet-pull-warble) has the diagnostic — capo at the fifth fret and play the same note; if the wobble eases, it is magnet pull and not a dead fret — and the line from it that applies directly here is that **higher-output pickups need more clearance, not less.** A factory height spec written for an Alnico 5 set is not a safe starting point for an Alnico 8 one.

So: install it, set it to whatever height the old pickup was at, play the open low E at the neck position, listen past the attack. If the note wavers or dies early, take the pickup down a half turn on each screw and listen again. Keep going until it stops. You will almost certainly end up lower than you expect, and the tone you lose from the extra distance is less than the tone you lose from a string the magnet is fighting.

## Four-Conductor Wiring, and What Splitting Costs You Through a Modeler

Every ELEV8 ships with four-conductor leads, so coil splitting and series/parallel switching are on the table without a rewind.

Worth knowing what you are actually getting. A split Alnico 8 humbucker is not a Stratocaster pickup — it is one coil of a humbucker, with a hum problem and a thinner voice, and our [single-coil vs. humbucker comparison](/blog/single-coil-vs-humbucker-worship-ambient-tone) covers where each one wins. What is genuinely useful about splitting a *hot* pickup is the range: a 15k Plus in series and one coil of it split are far further apart in level and character than a PAF and its split ever were.

That range is also the problem, and it is a modeler problem. Split a 15k pickup and you can drop 6 dB or more instantly, which on a tube amp reads as a clean-up and on a modeler reads as a preset that suddenly went quiet and thin. If you use the split as a real rhythm/lead switch, build for it: put the split position on its own snapshot with the amp Drive and channel volume compensated, or park a gain block after the input that comes on with it. Do not ask one set of amp settings to serve both ends of a 6 dB swing.

## Worth $139?

Against the field, yes, and I do not say that about Gibson pricing often.

$139 for an uncovered humbucker puts the ELEV8 right alongside Seymour Duncan and DiMarzio's standard line and under most boutique winders, and unlike a lot of Gibson's Pickup Shop catalogue, this one is not asking you to pay for a reissue story. It is a current-production pickup with a magnet Gibson has never used, four-conductor leads, and three complementary voices. The $10 premium for a nickel cover is the usual tax and it is fine.

Who should buy it: somebody with a Les Paul, SG or an import equivalent whose bridge pickup goes to mud in drop C, who wants more output without going to ceramic. The Plus-at-bridge, standard-at-neck pairing is the obvious set and it is the one Gibson designed toward.

Who should not: anybody chasing a PAF. This is not that pickup and it is not trying to be. If what you want is a 1959 bridge voice, Gibson already sells you four flavours of it and an Alnico 8 is the wrong end of the catalogue.

And whichever one you buy — set the height low, then pull an amp-block Drive knob back before you form an opinion. I have sold a lot of pickups to people who were actually returning a preset.
