Walrus Audio has relaunched both pedals in its Storybook Series as MKII versions. The **Lore** (2022) is its reverse soundscape pedal, and the **Fable** (March 2023) was the company's first granular delay. Premier Guitar ran the announcement on October 13, and [Guitar.com](https://guitar.com/news/gear-news/walrus-audio-revives-its-storybook-series-with-mkii-versions-of-its-much-loved-lore-and-fable-pedals/) has the fuller spec rundown.

This is what's new, per Walrus via [Premier Guitar](https://www.premierguitar.com/news/walrus-audio-announces-lore-stereo-reverse-soundscape-generator-fable-stereo-granular-soundscape-generator) and Guitar.com:

- **Mono-in / stereo-out** operation on both pedals. Neither source lists true stereo input.
- **MIDI control, clock sync, and expression/external tap**.
- **Three onboard presets plus a Live mode**, and **up to 127 presets over MIDI**.
- A multifunction **Tweak** control covering **Width, Mod Rate, Mod Shape, Shift and Ramp**.
- A momentary **Dive / Rise** function that bends the clock rate in real time.
- **Feedback and Regen** controls that send the sound through two separate feedback paths.
- Both pedals run on Walrus's in-house **MDSP** platform.
- **$349.99 / £319 each**, available now from Walrus dealers. Each comes in a Standard finish (Niall Grant artwork: Lore in green, Fable in gray) and a Minimal Black version.

**Lore MKII** has five dual-effect algorithms. They run from reverse delay into reverse reverb through to dual pitch-delay combinations. **Fable MKII** has five modes that pair delay with granular processing: reversed textures, octave-up cascades, octave-down ambience, glitchy clouds and randomized pitch. Walrus hasn't published formal mode names in either announcement I've read, so I won't make them up.

## The MIDI Is the Real Upgrade for a Worship Board

I'll say the unglamorous part first. The headline is stereo, but the change that decides whether these pedals make it onto a Sunday board is **127 MIDI presets plus clock sync**.

An ambient pedal like this has a lot of interacting controls: mix, time, feedback, regen, and now Tweak with five sub-parameters. You can't re-dial that between songs while the worship leader is talking into the next set. On the original pedals, a texture that worked in "Oceans" and a different one for the bridge of the next song meant either bending down mid-service or settling for one compromise setting all morning. On the MKII, the patch comes in with the song. If you already run a MIDI-capable switcher, or a modeler that can send program changes, each song in the rotation can have its own Lore or Fable patch next to its snapshot.

**Clock sync** matters for the same reason. Reverse delay and granular repeats sound musical when they land on the grid of the song. When they're a few milliseconds off the click, they sound like a mistake. With MIDI clock from the same source as your tracks, the repeats follow the tempo without anyone tapping.

## Stereo Out Is Great Until It Hits the Board

Here's the part the spec sheet doesn't tell you. Wide, randomized, pitch-scattered stereo is exactly the kind of image that **changes character when it's summed to mono**, and many church PAs run guitar mono, or close to it, front of house.

I covered how each stereo trick behaves when it's summed in [Which Stereo Widening Tricks Survive a Mono PA](/blog/stereo-width-tricks-that-survive-mono). Short version: content that's genuinely different on each side survives better than tricks built on tiny time offsets between the sides. Granular processing with randomized pitch should fall in the first group. A Width control set to maximum is the one to check. Before you set Tweak > Width by ear in your in-ears, run the thirty-second mono test from that piece: sum it, and listen to whether the texture thins out or gets a comb-filtered tilt.

On a board that's otherwise mono, place it with care, too. Mono-in / stereo-out means anything after it has to stay stereo, or the Lore or Fable's image collapses right back. If your reverb or modeler after it is stereo-capable, you're fine. If there's a mono block downstream, the stereo work is wasted. I cover that problem on Helix in [Helix Legacy Blocks Are Mono](/blog/helix-legacy-blocks-mono-stereo-collapse).

## Where These Sit in a Worship Chain, and How to Audition the Idea for Free

Both pedals are **delay-based** texture machines, not reverbs, so treat them that way: after drive, before your main reverb. Running Lore's reverse gesture into a short reverb is the same "reverse first, then smear it" ordering I recommend in [Reverse Reverb on Guitar](/blog/reverse-reverb-guitar-swells-and-textures).

If you play a Helix or Quad Cortex, you can test whether you'd actually use **Lore** tonight. Put the **Reverse Delay** block before a long reverb, set a longer reverse time and a moderate mix, and play the space between two chords of a slow song. That's the core gesture of the reverse modes. If you find yourself turning it off by the second chorus, Lore's other four algorithms probably won't change your mind.

**Fable** is harder to audition. I don't know of a single block on either platform that I'd call a stand-in for its granular modes. The octave-up and octave-down modes are the ones worth thinking about in a band, because they put new energy in octaves you might not own. If a keys player is holding a pad, read [Ambient Guitar and Keys Pad: Who Gets Which Octave](/blog/ambient-guitar-and-keys-pad-who-gets-which-octave) before you engage the octave-down cloud under their left hand.

## Dive / Rise: The Feature I Actually Want to Try

The **momentary Dive / Rise** bends the clock rate in real time, so the whole texture pitches and smears while you hold it. Most of what these pedals do is set-and-forget. This is the part that's *playable*, and I can hear its job right away: a transition. The last chord of one song rings out, you hold Dive, the wash sinks, and the next song's key comes in underneath. It's a bigger gesture than a volume swell, and it serves the room in the same way. Use it once per set, not once per song.

I haven't had either MKII on a board yet, so everything above is about the spec and how it fits into a service, not a review of the sound. For $349.99, the question isn't "does it sound amazing." Walrus ambient pedals usually do. The question is whether you'll recall it per song. With MIDI on board, you finally can.
