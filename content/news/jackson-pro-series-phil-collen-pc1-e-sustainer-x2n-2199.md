Jackson announced the **Pro Series Signature Phil Collen PC1-E** on **September 25**, and the headline is not the guitar, it's the line it landed in. Collen bought his first Jackson in 1986. His first signature model shipped in 1989. In the thirty-seven years since, there have been four of them — the PC Archtop, the PC1, the Custom Shop PC Supreme, and the PC1 Ninja — and every one has been a Custom Shop instrument.

**This is the first PC1 that isn't.** And it's **$2,199.99**.

Now, I ran a shop for twenty-five years, and I want to start there, because that number is going to confuse people who know the Pro Series.

## $2,199.99 Is Not a Pro Series Price

The Pro Series is Jackson's Indonesia-built tier. It normally lands somewhere between $1,000 and $1,600 depending on hardware, and the whole point of the line is "Custom Shop shape and playability, imported build, half the money." A PC1 from the Custom Shop will run you north of $5,000 if you can find one.

So $2,199.99 is the top of that line. It's the kind of price that makes a customer at my counter go quiet and then ask, reasonably, what am I paying for. Here's the honest answer, and it's two things.

**One: the Jackson Sustainer System, at 18 volts, with a harmonic/fundamental blend switch.** That is an active electronics package occupying the neck pickup position, with its own battery compartment and its own driver coil. This is not a pickup. It is a small piece of electromechanical engineering, and it costs real money to build and install. On the used market, sustainer installs alone run several hundred dollars plus labor, and a factory install done right is worth more than an aftermarket one because the routing and shielding were planned rather than improvised. Our [Sustainiac versus Fernandes install guide](/blog/sustainer-pickup-sustainiac-vs-fernandes-install-for-live-lead) walks through what that job actually involves.

**Two: a Floyd Rose 1000 Series with a 37mm block, plus Jackson locking tuners.** The 1000 Series is the good imported Floyd — German-spec tolerances, and the one I'd actually set up without complaining. The **37mm block** is the part worth noting: it's a deeper sustain block than you normally see at this price, and a deeper block genuinely changes how the bridge couples to the body. It also means the trem cavity is routed for it, so you can't cheaply swap in something shallower later — not that you'd want to. If you've never lived with a Floyd, read [the setup guide](/blog/floyd-rose-setup-guide) before you order, not after.

The rest of the spec sheet is good and unremarkable for the money: genuine mahogany body, one-piece caramelized maple neck with **dual graphite reinforcement rods**, ebony fingerboard with a **compound 12"–16" radius**, 24 jumbo frets, 25.5" scale, pearloid sharkfin at the 12th, gold hardware throughout, and a foam core case.

The **distressed nitrocellulose lacquer** in Worn Black is the one I'd flag for anyone thinking of this as an investment. A factory-relic'd nitro finish on a signature model is a narrow resale market — you're buying someone else's decision about where the wear goes, permanently. That's not a criticism of the guitar. It's an appraisal. Buy this because you want to play it.

## The Pickups Are Going to Break Your Presets

Now the part that matters if you play through a modeler, which is most of you.

The bridge pickup is a **DiMarzio X2N**. This is not a normal humbucker. The X2N is one of the highest-output passive pickups anyone has ever put in a production guitar — a ceramic-magnet, twin-blade design that has been the reference for "absurdly hot" since 1979. The middle is a **DiMarzio Sugar Chakra**, a much more moderate single-coil-sized unit, with push-pull coil splitting available.

Here is what that combination does to a rig that was dialed in on anything else.

**Your amp block's Drive is now too high.** Not by a little. An X2N is delivering dramatically more signal into your modeler's input than an Alnico 5 PAF-style humbucker, and a high-gain amp model's input stage responds to that the same way a real one does: it compresses earlier, the attack transient flattens, and the note loses its front edge. If you build a preset on a Les Paul and then plug in a PC1-E, **take 1.0 to 1.5 off the amp block's Drive before you judge anything.** If it still sounds like mush, take another point off.

**Your noise gate threshold is now wrong in the other direction.** A hotter pickup raises both signal and noise floor, so a threshold that was correctly placed is now clipping note tails. Re-set it with the guitar you're actually going to use — [threshold and decay for high gain](/blog/noise-gate-threshold-decay-settings-high-gain) covers how to find it without guessing.

**And the ceramic magnet is pulling harder on your strings.** This is the part nobody checks. A ceramic magnet at a given height exerts more pull on the string than an Alnico 2 or 3 at the same height, which affects sustain and intonation stability — and on a Floyd-equipped guitar, string pull interacts with your spring tension. Our piece on [Alnico 2, 3, 5 and ceramic magnets and string pull](/blog/pickup-magnet-types-alnico-2-3-5-ceramic-string-pull) has the measurements. If the guitar arrives and the low strings sound wobbly on sustained notes, lower the bridge pickup a full turn before you blame the setup.

## The Sustainer Is the Thing Your Modeler Cannot Do

I want to be direct about this because it's the actual answer to "why buy the guitar instead of modeling it."

Every other feature on this instrument has a digital equivalent or near-equivalent. Amp models cover the amp. The X2N's output is a gain-staging change you can approximate with an input trim. The Floyd is a playing technique.

**The sustainer is not a signal-processing effect. It is a feedback loop through the physical string.** A driver coil in the neck position pushes the string while the bridge pickup listens, and the string keeps vibrating indefinitely — with a **harmonic/fundamental blend switch** here, meaning you can choose whether the note holds at its own pitch or blooms into an upper harmonic. There is no block in a Helix, an HX Stomp, a Quad Cortex, a Fractal or a Kemper that does this, because the thing being sustained is a steel string in a magnetic field, not a waveform in a buffer.

What you *can* do without one, and what I'd suggest if $2,199.99 is not happening this year, is [an EBow](/blog/ebow-guitar-technique-infinite-sustain-swells) — same physical principle, handheld, about seventy dollars, and it does violin-like swells the sustainer doesn't. It won't switch on with your foot and it occupies your picking hand. But it is the honest budget answer, and I'd rather tell you that than pretend a compressor and a long reverb tail get you there. They don't.

## One Last Thing About Def Leppard Tone

If you're buying this to chase *Hysteria*, I need to correct something, because I watched people get this wrong across my counter for two decades.

That record is not a high-gain record. The guitars on it are **layered** — many takes, stacked, panned wide, heavily compressed, and processed at the desk — and each individual guitar in that stack is far less distorted than the finished wall sounds. Collen and Steve Clark were tracking parts that are close to a cranked crunch, not a modern scooped metal tone, and the size comes from arrangement and mixing.

So if you plug an X2N into your most aggressive preset and it sounds nothing like the record, that's the reason, and it isn't the guitar's fault. Back the Drive down until a single track sounds thinner than you expect. Then double it, pan the two takes hard left and right, and listen again. That's where the sound lives — same principle as the layering work in our [A/B amp switching for verse and chorus](/blog/ab-amp-switching-modeler-verse-chorus) piece.

Thirty-seven years is a long collaboration. This is a well-specified guitar with one feature you genuinely cannot get any other way. Just don't buy it expecting the relic finish to appreciate.
