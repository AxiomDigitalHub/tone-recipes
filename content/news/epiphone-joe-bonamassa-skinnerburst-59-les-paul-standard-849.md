Epiphone announced the Joe Bonamassa Skinnerburst '59 Les Paul Standard on November 18. It's an $849 (£949) take on the 1959 Burst that Bonamassa says he has "basically used on every record since" he bought it. Gibson Custom built a Skinnerburst replica back in 2014, limited to 300 guitars, and those now trade in five figures. This version costs less than a good used Deluxe Reverb.

I'm not a Burst person. My guitars are a Jazzmaster and a semi-hollow. But Bonamassa's neck-pickup lead sound is one of the few rock tones that sounds as good at living-room volume as it does on a stage, and that's the part worth talking about here.

## Where the Name Comes From

A family patriarch in Benton, Minnesota, bought the guitar new in 1959. Bonamassa says it cost $265 without the case. It passed to his son around 1980. In 2006 the son, about to retire as a police chief, sold it through the Skinner auction house. Guitar World puts the sale at $292,000, which made it the most expensive non-celebrity guitar sold at auction at the time. Bonamassa remembers the number as "something like $337,000." The gap is probably the buyer's premium, but I haven't confirmed that. He bought the guitar from the auction winner later on.

The finish is named after the auction house. Epiphone calls it Dirty Lemon, a faded burst with a faded cherry back.

## What's in the Box

From the launch coverage (Guitar World and MusicRadar, both November 18):

- **Body:** mahogany with a maple cap and flame maple veneer
- **Neck:** mahogany, **Custom '59 rounded C** profile
- **Fingerboard:** rosewood, 22 medium-jumbo frets, trapezoid pearloid inlays
- **Nut:** Graph Tech
- **Headstock:** Kalamazoo-style
- **Electronics:** CTS pots and Mallory capacitors
- **Hardware:** LockTone Tune-O-Matic and Stop Bar, Epiphone Deluxe tuners with keystone buttons
- **Case:** brown-and-pink hardshell with Nerdville graphics on the outside
- **Price:** $849 / £949, listed as a limited edition and available now

**The pickups are where the reports disagree.** Guitar World lists a ProBucker in the bridge and a ProBucker 3 in the neck. MusicRadar lists a ProBucker 3 and a ProBucker Custom and doesn't say which goes where. Neither report gives magnet or output specs. If the pickups matter to you (and for this sound they should), read the spec sheet on Epiphone's own listing or ask your dealer before you pay.

**The edition size isn't confirmed either.** One retailer lists 1,500 guitars, plus a 300-piece version with a signature inlay sold through Bonamassa's own store. Neither trade report gives a number, so treat 1,500 as one dealer's figure. Stock varies between dealers, and at least one already shows it sold out.

## Getting the Skinnerburst Sound on Your Rig

Our [Sloe Gin recipe](/recipe/bonamassa-sloe-gin-blues-rock-lead) is the tone I'd start from. It's built around a '59 Les Paul and the creamy, singing neck-pickup lead that most people mean when they say "Bonamassa tone." Most of it carries over to whatever humbucker guitar you already own.

**1. It's a neck-pickup tone.** The recipe specs the **neck pickup**, not the bridge. Most people reach for the bridge for leads out of habit. Bonamassa's sustain comes from the neck pickup's thicker fundamental pushing a mid-gain amp, so the notes sing without getting fizzy. If you're on the bridge and wondering why your version sounds thin and spiky, that's the first thing to change.

**2. The Tube Screamer is a push, not a distortion.** The recipe runs an always-on TS808 at **Drive 3, Tone 5.5, Level 7** on Helix (Scream 808). The low drive and high level hit the amp harder without adding much clipping of its own. You also get the TS mid hump, and that's what keeps a neck humbucker from turning woolly. Our [Tube Screamer settings guide](/blog/tube-screamer-settings-guide) explains why this low-drive, high-level setting works the way it does.

**3. The amp is a Dumble, not a Plexi.** On Helix the recipe uses the **Cartographer** (Line 6's Dumble-style model) at **Drive 6, Bass 5, Mid 6, Treble 6, Presence 5.5, Master 10**, into a **4x12 Greenback 25**. Bonamassa often blends a Dumble with a Marshall. On a single-amp preset, the Dumble model gives you the transparency and the Greenbacks give you the British midrange. The full recipe also has Quad Cortex, Fractal, Katana and TONEX translations.

**4. Set the drive for your pickup, not his.** We don't know the output of these ProBuckers yet, and we don't know what your current humbuckers put out either. If your guitar has hot modern pickups, start the Cartographer Drive closer to 5 and let the TS do the pushing. Our [overdrive-with-humbuckers guide](/blog/overdrive-with-humbuckers-settings) explains how pickup output moves where those knobs should land.

**5. The guitar's volume knob does the rest.** CTS pots are a good sign on an Epiphone at this price, because a smooth taper is what makes rolling back the volume useful. Set the amp so the neck pickup sings at full volume, then roll back to about 7 for rhythm parts. That one-amp, two-sounds approach is how a blues player gets dynamics without tap-dancing on a pedalboard. Our [volume knob guide](/blog/one-amp-two-sounds-volume-knob-settings) covers the details.

One thing the recipe specs that's easy to skip: **.011–.052 strings**. Heavier strings give the neck pickup more fundamental to work with, and that thickness is a lot of what makes Bonamassa's sustain sound effortless.

## Should You Buy It?

If you want a '59-spec Les Paul with a rounded neck, decent electronics and a great case for under $900, this is a good deal before you even count the name on the headstock. If you already own a humbucker guitar you love, put it on the neck pickup, put 11s on it, push a Dumble model with a low-drive TS808, and play the solo quietly enough that you have to dig in. That gets you most of the way there. The Dirty Lemon burst is the part you can't add with a preset.
