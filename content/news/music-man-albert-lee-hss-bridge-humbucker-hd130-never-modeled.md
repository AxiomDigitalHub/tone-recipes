Ernie Ball Music Man announced two new **Albert Lee** models on **October 7**.

The **Albert Lee HSS** is **$3,299** street. It keeps the unmistakable Albert Lee body — that offset shape with the long upper horn that looks like nothing else on a wall — and changes the pickups:

| Position | Pickup |
|---|---|
| 1 | Neck single coil |
| 2 | Neck + middle |
| 3 | Middle single coil |
| 4 | Middle + bridge humbucker |
| **5** | **Bridge humbucker alone** |

The humbucker is a **DiMarzio custom originally developed for the Music Man Axis**. The two single coils are **Albert Lee's signature Seymour Duncan custom winds**, the same family as the long-running SSS model. An onboard preamp carries Music Man's **Silent Circuit**, which the company describes as reducing 60-cycle hum *and* helping "normalize output across all five pickup selector positions." Hold onto that second clause.

The rest: **ash body** in high-gloss polyester, **roasted figured maple neck**, 25-1/2" scale, **22 stainless steel frets** (high profile, medium width), compensated nut, Music Man vintage tremolo, **Schaller M6-IND locking tuners**, glow-in-the-dark side dots. Finishes are Honey Burst, Firemist Silver and Cosmos Sparkle.

Alongside it: a **Ball Family Reserve Albert Lee in Malibu Burst, limited to 75 instruments worldwide** — figured maple top over an **okoume** body, custom-wound DiMarzio humbuckers (this one is a dual-humbucker guitar, not an HSS), white pearl Atlante block inlays, roasted figured maple neck *and* fretboard, 10" radius. No price was announced for the BFR.

So that is the announcement. Now I want to talk about the bridge pickup, because I think the coverage is going to call this "the most versatile Albert Lee yet" and move on, and there is a genuinely interesting tension here that nobody is going to name.

## The Position His Entire Sound Lives In

Albert Lee is, arguably, the most consequential hybrid picker alive. Emmylou Harris's Hot Band. Eric Clapton's band from 1979 to 1983. The Everly Brothers. "Country Boy." If you have ever heard a guitar part where the pick and two bare fingers are doing three different rhythmic jobs at once and it still swings, you have heard his descendants.

That sound is a **bridge single-coil** sound. Not incidentally — structurally.

We have been blunt about this in our own guide to the [Nashville session clean tone](/blog/nashville-session-clean-tele-compressor), and I will quote us against ourselves here: *"A humbucker gets you none of it."* The snap that defines the style is a narrow-aperture, tightly-wound single coil sensing a very short length of string right at the bridge, with a hard pick transient and a mid-forward decay. A humbucker senses a longer span of string, averages across it, and cancels the exact high-frequency content the technique depends on. That is not a taste judgment, it is a geometry one.

The original Albert Lee was an **SSS** — three Seymour Duncan custom winds. There has been an **HH** in the line for years, a two-humbucker variant. The HSS splits the difference, and the split lands the humbucker in **position five**.

I do not think this is a mistake, and I want to be careful here, because "signature guitar gets a spec its namesake would not have asked for" is a cheap story and it is usually wrong. Lee has played the HH for years. The man is 83 and has nothing left to prove about chicken pickin'.

But I think a lot of people are going to buy this guitar, flip to position five, plug into a country preset, and wonder why it does not do the thing.

So: **the Albert Lee tone on this guitar is position three.** Middle single coil, alone. Possibly position two if you want it rounder. Position four — middle plus bridge humbucker — is a real and useful sound that is not quite either thing, and position five is a rock guitar. If you want to know why position choice does this much work, the mechanics are in our [pickup position guide](/blog/pickup-position-guide), and the technique that has to come first either way is in [chicken pickin' foundations](/blog/chicken-pickin-foundations) and the [four hybrid-picking moves](/blog/hybrid-picking-exercises-four-moves) that work before your right hand is ready.

That is the gear-press version of the story. Here is the part I actually got excited about.

## The Silent Circuit Spec Is the Best Thing on This Guitar for Modeler Players

Go back to that phrase: Silent Circuit **"helps normalize output across all five pickup selector positions."**

If you play through an amp, that is a convenience. If you play through a modeler, it is a structural fix for a problem that has annoyed me for years.

An HSS guitar is a gain-staging nightmare in a digital rig. A hot humbucker and a vintage-output single coil can sit **5 to 8 dB apart**, and in front of a real amp that difference is mostly loudness — the amp's input stage is forgiving and your volume knob does the rest. In front of a modeled amp it is not just loudness, because **the amp model's breakup point is a function of input level.** Switch from position three to position five mid-song and you do not get "the same tone, louder." You get a different operating point on the gain curve: more compression, earlier saturation, a different top end. Your clean preset stops being clean.

This is the whole subject of [one modeler, two guitars, different output levels](/blog/one-modeler-two-guitars-different-output-levels), and the usual fixes are all compromises — a gain block that switches with the pickup, a per-snapshot input trim, or just accepting it.

A guitar that arrives with its five positions already level-matched **removes the problem at the source.** One preset, one gain structure, five usable sounds, and the amp model behaves the same in all of them. I have not played this guitar and I am not going to pretend the normalization is perfect. But as a design goal it is the single most modeler-friendly thing in the announcement, and it is in a sentence about hum reduction that everyone is going to skim.

One honest caveat: level-matching is level-matching, not voicing-matching. Position five will still be a humbucker. It will just be a humbucker that does not kick your amp model into a different gain regime on the way through.

## The Amp Nobody Has Modeled

Now the part I had to go verify three times before I believed it.

Albert Lee's amp — the one he has used for decades, the one on the records — is a **Music Man HD-130**. And in a quote collected on Equipboard he says he is *"still using my old 130 watt music man amps."*

**No major modeler has it.** Not Line 6. Not Fractal. Not Neural. Not Kemper's factory profiles. There are wishlist threads on the Fractal forum going back years, and the recurring explanation from the community is that the HD-130 has a **solid-state front end**, which sits outside what Fractal's amp modeling is built around.

That explanation is correct, and it is also the most useful fact in this entire article, because the architecture tells you what to do.

The HD-130 was Leo Fender and Tom Walker's post-CBS project, built 1974–1979, aimed squarely at out-loud-ing a Twin Reverb. Its signal path is genuinely strange:

- A **solid-state preamp** — no preamp tubes doing tone work at all
- A single **12AX7 as the phase inverter**
- **Four 6CA7** output tubes
- Plates running **upward of 700 volts DC**, which is extreme — a Twin Reverb is in the 400s
- **65 or 130 watts**, with a half-power switch that works by **dropping B+** rather than by switching out two tubes

Read that list as a tone description and it says one thing: **this amp has essentially no preamp character and a vast amount of headroom, and every bit of compression it has ever produced came from the power stage.** A solid-state preamp does not soften, does not sag, does not add the harmonic haze a 12AX7 gain stage adds. And at 700 volts on the plates, the output tubes are being held in a state of brutal linearity — which is exactly why Leo built it, because he was chasing the cleanest loud amp anyone had made.

That is also why it is a country player's amp. You do not want preamp coloration under a chicken-pickin' part. You want a wire with a speaker on the end of it and a compressor doing the shaping.

## The Three Settings That Get You Close

You cannot load an HD-130 model. You can reproduce its *behavior*, and because the architecture is so lopsided, there are only three things to change.

**1. Start from a Twin Reverb model and take the Drive to the floor.** On Helix that is **US Double Nrm** (Fender Twin Reverb, normal channel), which our [Helix amp model cheat sheet](/blog/helix-amp-model-cheat-sheet) lists at a 25% starting gain as the maximum-clean-headroom model in the box. Take it lower. Go to **1.5–2.0** and leave it there. Every bit of preamp gain you dial in is modeling a tube stage the HD-130 does not have. Helix amp blocks run a **0–10 scale, not 0–1**, so 2.0 means two, not two-tenths.

**2. Push the Master, hard, and make up the level downstream.** In a Helix amp block the **Master parameter models the power section**, not your output volume. This is the move that matters, because on an HD-130 the power section is the *only* nonlinear thing in the amp. Bring Master to **7.0–8.0** and pull the block Level or your Output block down to compensate. You are buying power-stage compression with no preamp distortion stacked on top of it — which is an operating point almost nobody dials in, because it feels backwards. If you cannot hear which stage is doing the work, [preamp vs power amp distortion and how to tell them apart](/blog/preamp-vs-power-amp-distortion-how-to-hear) is the ear-training.

**3. Scoop nothing, and let the compressor do the shaping.** The HD-130's headroom means the amp is not making your midrange decisions. A compressor in front is. Our settings there are specific and I stand behind them: **ratio 3:1 to 4:1, fast attack, release around noon, threshold below your peak picking level, makeup gain at or just above unity**. The full reasoning — including why a slow attack sounds more natural and destroys the style — is in the [Nashville session clean tone](/blog/nashville-session-clean-tele-compressor) guide and the [chicken pickin' compressor settings](/blog/chicken-pickin-compressor-settings) breakdown. If your country tone already sounds squashed and lifeless, the odds are very good it is [the compressor ruining it, not the amp](/blog/compressor-ruining-country-tone), and on a modeler [where you place that block changes what it does](/blog/compressor-placement-modeler-preset-pre-amp-post-amp).

For the cab: a 2x12 or 4x12 with the kind of high-headroom American voicing the HD-130 was built around. If you are reaching for a Deluxe Reverb instead because that is what you own, our [Deluxe Reverb settings guide](/blog/fender-deluxe-reverb-settings) will get you a sweeter, earlier-breaking version of the same idea — just know you are modeling a 22-watt amp doing the opposite of what a 130-watt one does.

And the six settings that go wrong most often in this style, almost all of them upstream of the amp, are in [country Telecaster tone: the 6 settings players get wrong](/blog/country-telecaster-tone-settings).

## A Quote I Cannot Resolve, and I Would Rather Say So

In that same Equipboard entry, Lee is quoted saying he has *"been very happy with a fender tone master."*

I want to flag that I genuinely cannot tell which amp he means, and the difference is funny enough to be worth the paragraph.

**Fender has made two completely unrelated things called Tone-Master.** There is the **1993–2000 Custom Shop Tone-Master**, a point-to-point 100-watt two-channel 6L6 tube head that Bruce Zinky designed when Fender asked him to build something that could fight a Marshall. And there is the **modern Tone Master line**, which is digital — the [Tone Master '59 Twin-Amp JB Edition that Fender and Joe Bonamassa announced two days ago](/news/fender-bonamassa-tone-master-59-twin-jb-5f8a-quad-cortex-helix) is the newest one.

An undated quote from a player who was gigging heavily in 1995 could easily be the Zinky amp. It could equally be a 2020s digital combo. I am not going to decide for him, and I am slightly delighted by the ambiguity, because one reading means the HD-130 guy settled on a boutique tube head and the other means he has quietly been playing a modeler for years while his actual amp remains the only one in his signal chain nobody has bothered to capture.

## What I Would Want to Measure

Two things, and both are cheap for anyone who has the gear.

First: **a sweep of all five HSS positions at matched picking force**, published as levels in dB. Music Man says Silent Circuit normalizes output across the selector. I believe the intent; I want the number. If the spread is inside 2 dB, this guitar is a meaningfully better modeler guitar than any HSS on the market and should be sold that way. If it is 5 dB, it is a hum-canceling circuit with a nice sentence attached.

Second: **an HD-130 capture.** It is a NAM profile and an afternoon. The amp has four tubes, one preamp board, and a tone that a generation of country and roots players built their sound on, and the reason it has never been modeled is apparently that its front end is made of the wrong material. That is a reason to profile it, not a reason to skip it.

I do not have the guitar or the amp, so both of those are things I want rather than things I know. But the settings above are the ones I would start from tonight, and the pickup position is three.
