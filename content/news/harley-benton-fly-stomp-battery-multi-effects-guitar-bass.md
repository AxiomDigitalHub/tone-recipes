Harley Benton put out the FLY-STOMP on November 19. It's a metal box with three footswitches and a battery inside, and it comes in two versions: an orange guitar unit and a lilac bass unit. Thomann lists them at **$54 for the guitar** and **$63 for the bass** on its US storefront (€59 and €69 in Europe, £49 and £58 in the UK). Both show as in stock now.

That's less than most single overdrive pedals. So the real question isn't whether it's cheap. It's what you're actually getting, and whether it's the right thing for your board.

## What's in the Guitar Version

From Harley Benton's launch post and the Thomann listing:

- **Drive (9 types):** Green 9, Yellow-D, Boost RC, Boost AC, Muff, Face Fuzz, Dr. Blues, Klone, Crunchist
- **Delay (3 types):** Analog, Tape Echo, Digital, all with tap tempo
- **Reverb (3 types):** Studio, Spring, Concert, plus a **Freeze** function
- **Noise gate:** built in
- **Footswitches:** three, with status LEDs
- **I/O:** 1/4" input, **stereo out** (2× 1/4"), USB-C for charging
- **Power:** 500 mAh lithium battery, "over five hours" of playtime per Harley Benton, 85 mA draw. A USB-C to USB-A cable comes in the box, a power supply doesn't
- **Size:** 200 × 80 × 52 mm, 535 g

## What's in the Bass Version

The bass unit is the more complete package:

- **Amp sims:** three (Mes 400, DP 751, SV Bass)
- **Cab sims:** three (2×12, 8×10, 4×10), with a 3-band EQ and sweepable mid
- **Comp** with a sustain control, **Dist** with Drive, Dist and Fuzz types, and **Mod** with chorus, flanger and phaser (tap tempo)
- **Tuner** built in
- **I/O:** 1/4" in, **balanced XLR out** plus 1/4" out, 3.5 mm aux in, 3.5 mm headphone out, and USB audio for recording

Same battery, size and weight as the guitar unit.

## The Catch on the Guitar Version

The guitar FLY-STOMP has **no amp or cab simulation** in the launch description, and **no headphone out**. Thomann's spec table says "Amp Modeling: Yes" for the guitar unit, but that contradicts the module list from both Thomann and Harley Benton, so I'd assume there isn't any until someone shows otherwise. That makes it a dirt/delay/reverb pedalboard in one box. It still needs an amp, or a modeler's amp block, after it. Plug it straight into a PA or headphones and you'll get raw fuzzbox fizz.

The bass version is the opposite. With amp, cab, XLR and a headphone jack, it can actually run straight to the board. If you play bass and need a direct box for church or a bar gig, that's the more interesting unit here.

Nobody has published NAM support, IR loading, an app, MIDI or a preset count for either one. Assume it's knobs and footswitches.

## Reading the Drive Names

Harley Benton doesn't say what each drive is based on, but the names follow the usual clone-naming habits. Here's my best guess at each one. It's a guess, not a confirmed spec:

| FLY-STOMP name | Probably points at | Where to start |
|---|---|---|
| Green 9 | TS9-style Tube Screamer | Drive low, level high, into an amp that's already on the edge |
| Klone | Klon Centaur | Gain under noon, treble around noon, level up as a clean-ish push |
| Muff | Big Muff Pi | Sustain past noon, tone just under noon so it doesn't go to mush |
| Face Fuzz | Fuzz Face | Guitar volume does the work, so roll it back to clean up |
| Boost AC / Boost RC | Xotic AC / RC Booster | AC for a grittier push, RC for a cleaner one |
| Dr. Blues | Blues Driver-style | Low gain, the tone knob is touchy |
| Yellow-D | A yellow distortion (Distortion+ type, likely) | Treat it as a mid-gain hard clipper |
| Crunchist | Not obvious | Play it before you assume anything |

The "where to start" column comes from our own pedal guides, not from testing this box. On the Green 9, the [Tube Screamer settings guide](/blog/tube-screamer-settings-guide) explains why low drive and high level is the setting that works in front of a driven amp. On the Klone, the [Klon settings guide](/blog/klon-centaur-settings-guide) covers why the original sounds best as a boost rather than a distortion. The [Big Muff settings guide](/blog/big-muff-settings-guide) is the one to read before you push the Muff's tone knob anywhere near max.

One drive slot means **one drive at a time**. You can't stack the Klone into the Muff the way you would on a real board. If stacking is your thing, this isn't your box.

## Who Should Buy It

**The backup board.** I keep a RAT and a DS-1 under my HX Stomp because I don't trust anything I can't fix with a screwdriver at 11 PM. A battery box with a drive, a delay and a reverb is a better backup than two dirt pedals, as long as the venue has an amp you can plug into. Our [post on keeping old pedals as backups](/blog/old-budget-pedals-after-upgrade-resale-backup) makes the case for this.

**The fly date.** The name gives it away. A 535 g box with no power supply to pack fits in a gig bag pocket, and five hours of battery covers a soundcheck and a set with plenty left over. Just remember the guitar version needs a real amp at the other end.

**Not for silent practice.** No headphone jack on the guitar unit means it can't replace a headphone amp. If that's what you need, look at our [Katana Mini vs. Vox amPlug comparison](/blog/katana-mini-vs-vox-amplug-4-budget-headphone).

**Bass players should look first.** XLR out, amp and cab sims, a tuner and a headphone jack for $63 is a much stronger deal than the guitar version.

## The Bottom Line

The guitar FLY-STOMP is nine drives, three delays, three reverbs and a gate in a battery box for about the price of a used RAT. It isn't a modeler and doesn't pretend to be. The tap-tempo delays and the reverb Freeze are nice extras at this price. Losing drive stacking and a headphone jack is the trade-off for the size. Whether the Klone sounds anything like a Klon is something nobody can tell you until these get into players' hands. Don't buy it for that one sound.
