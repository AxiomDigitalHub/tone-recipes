Gibson announced the Victory Baritone today: a 28-inch-scale, B-to-B double-cut with two brand-new ELEV8 humbuckers, at **$2,499** including a matching hardshell case, in Graphite Metallic and British Racing Green.

I sold a fair number of guitars in twenty-five years behind a counter in Austin, and I want to tell you about the last time Gibson tried this shape, because it makes this announcement a lot more interesting than the spec sheet does on its own.

## The Victory Was a Flop, and That's Why It's Worth Something

The original Victory came out in 1981 — deep Norlin era, Kalamazoo-built, Gibson watching Fender and every hot-rod builder in California eat their lunch and deciding to answer with a double-cutaway. Two models: the **Victory MV-II**, two humbuckers designed by Tim Shaw, rosewood board, three-way switch; and the **Victory MV-X**, HSH configuration, five-way switch, ebony board.

They lasted until 1983, maybe into '84 depending on whose serial records you believe. They did not sell. Gibson players wanted Les Pauls and the people who wanted a superstrat were not shopping at Gibson dealers, and the Victory landed in the gap between the two and stayed there.

Which is exactly why the ones that survived are collectible now. Short production run, decent build, genuinely good Tim Shaw pickups, and a shape nobody else made. I bought and sold a handful of MV-IIs over the years and the good ones always surprised the person who played them. Gibson brought the shape back in 2024 as a flame-topped two-humbucker guitar, and this baritone is the second act.

## What They Actually Built

Twenty-eight inches of scale, tuned B to B — a fourth below standard, sitting between a guitar and a bass. Guitar World is calling it Gibson's first baritone in about a decade, and that squares with what I remember of the catalog.

Double-cutaway mahogany body, and here's a detail I like: **no pickguard, electronics mounted from the rear.** Clean face. Glued-in mahogany neck with a rounded profile, compound-radius ebony fretboard that flattens from 10 inches at the nut to 16 at the top, 24 medium jumbo frets, acrylic dots. Explorer-style headstock, Graph Tech nut, Grover Mini Rotomatics, Tune-o-matic bridge.

The pickups are the headline. Two new **Gibson ELEV8 humbuckers** built around **Alnico 8** magnets with custom-gauge wiring, and the push/pulls do something more interesting than the usual coil-split: you get split single-coil operation *and* selection between the **inner or outer coils.**

## Alnico 8 Is a Deliberate Choice, and It's the Right One Here

Let me be plain about what that magnet does, because "Alnico 8" gets thrown around as though it just means "hot."

Alnico 8 has substantially more magnetic energy than the Alnico 2 in a '59-style PAF or the Alnico 5 in most standard humbuckers. Higher output, a firmer and more aggressive low end, a compressed and forward attack, and less of that loose vintage bloom. It's a magnet people reach for when they want authority rather than sweetness. I broke down how the different magnet types behave — including the string-pull problem hot magnets create — in [pickup magnet types: Alnico 2, 3, 5, and ceramic](/blog/pickup-magnet-types-alnico-2-3-5-ceramic-string-pull), and that string-pull note matters more than usual here. Heavier baritone strings over a strong magnet at close pickup height is exactly the recipe for warbly, wolfy intonation. Set the pickups **lower than you would on a Les Paul** and let the output take care of itself.

Why Alnico 8 is correct on a baritone specifically: a low B fundamental is **61.74 Hz**, versus 82.41 Hz for a standard low E. A softer vintage magnet on a heavy, slack, low-tuned string gives you a woolly, indistinct attack with no pick definition. A stronger magnet keeps the transient sharp and gives the note an edge to hang onto. Gibson made a functional decision, not a marketing one.

## Now the Part About Your Modeler

Most people buying this will play it through a Helix, a Quad Cortex, or something like them. Here's what changes.

**Your amp model choice shifts down the gain ladder, not up.** The instinct with a hot pickup is to reach for more gain. Do the opposite. Alnico 8 humbuckers into a high-gain model at B-to-B tuning produces mush — you've got more output, more low-frequency energy, and more magnet compression all arriving at the distortion stage at once. Start with a lower-gain amp than you think you want and let the pickup do the work. The [Helix amp model cheat sheet](/blog/helix-amp-model-cheat-sheet) is the fastest way to find the models that stay articulate under a hot input, and [10 underrated Helix amp models](/blog/10-helix-amp-models-underrated) is where I'd look for a low-gain amp with enough iron in the low end to handle this.

**Your drive pedal settings are wrong out of the box.** Almost every overdrive preset in circulation was dialed with a passive Alnico 5 humbucker or a single-coil in mind. With Alnico 8 into the same block, you're clipping earlier and harder, and the fix is Drive down, Level up — the same balance argument I make in [overdrive settings with humbuckers](/blog/overdrive-with-humbuckers-settings) and, for the specific pedals, [TS808, Klon, and RAT with humbuckers](/blog/ts808-klon-rat-humbuckers).

**Your cab block is the weak link.** A 61.7 Hz fundamental through a 4x12 IR captured with a modern speaker is going to get thinned out — those speakers roll off below roughly 80 Hz by design. You have real options here and they're covered in [Helix cab models decoded](/blog/helix-cab-models-decoded) and [Helix cab and IR pairings](/blog/helix-cab-ir-pairings). What I'd try: a larger-format cab IR, or a 4x12 blended with something that extends lower, low-passed so it only adds weight. And know that this is a cabinet-size problem before it's a modeling problem — [why cabinet size matters](/blog/cabinet-volume-and-tone-why-size-matters) is the physics behind it.

**Your EQ moves down with the tuning.** If you have a stock mid boost sitting at 800 Hz because that's where it lives on a guitar in E, shift it. Everything that defines "this note" moved down a fourth. The framework is in [the modeler EQ guide](/blog/modeler-eq-guide) and, for the underlying theory, [the guitar EQ guide](/blog/guitar-eq-guide).

## The Coil Switching Is the Sleeper Feature

I want to spend a paragraph on the inner/outer coil selection, because it's the thing most reviews will list and not explain.

A humbucker has two coils sitting about an inch apart. On a Les Paul that inch translates into a real difference — the coil closer to the bridge hears a brighter, sharper slice of the string; the one closer to the neck hears a fuller one. Splitting to the inner coil of a bridge humbucker gets you something noticeably rounder and more Strat-middle than splitting to the outer coil, which lands closer to a hot Tele bridge.

On a **28-inch scale**, that inch of coil spacing represents a smaller fraction of the string length, so the difference is subtler than on a short-scale guitar — but the payoff is different too. Split single-coil tones on a baritone are genuinely useful for clean work where a full humbucker at low B just gets congested. If you're deciding when to use which, the same reasoning I laid out in [single-coil vs. humbucker for clean and ambient tones](/blog/single-coil-vs-humbucker-worship-ambient-tone) applies, and [the pickup position guide](/blog/pickup-position-guide) covers what moving along the string does to the harmonic content.

Practical starting point: **outer coil split on the bridge for clean rhythm, full humbucker for anything with dirt, inner coil split on the neck for anything where you want a note to sit in a mix without dominating it.** Then ignore me and find your own.

## Whether It's Worth $2,499

Two and a half thousand dollars buys you a lot of guitar in 2026, and Gibson has priced this at Les Paul Standard money for a shape most people have never played.

What you're paying for is real: an ebony board, a compound radius, a glued neck, Graph Tech and Grover hardware, a case, and a genuinely new pickup design rather than a rebadge. What you're betting on is that a 28-inch B-to-B Gibson is an instrument you'll reach for rather than a novelty that lives in the case. I've watched a lot of baritones and 12-strings come back through my shop on consignment six months after purchase, and the ones that stayed sold were bought by people who already knew exactly what part they needed it for.

If you don't have that part in mind yet, wait for the used market. It won't be long.

The historical footnote, though, is genuinely fun. Gibson took a guitar that failed in 1981 because it was chasing a trend, brought it back in 2024, and has now used it as the platform for the one thing the original was never going to be: a long-scale, heavy-magnet, downtuned instrument aimed squarely at players who weren't born when the first one was discontinued. That's a better second act than most Norlin-era guitars get.
