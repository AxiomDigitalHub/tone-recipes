Fender announced the **Squier Paranormal Troy Van Leeuwen Telecaster XII** on **September 8**. It's **$599.99**, it's available now, and it's the **first signature model ever released in the Paranormal series** — that's the line Squier uses for the mash-ups and what-ifs, the offset-body Teles and the 12-strings that never existed in the catalog.

The specs, as published:

- **Nyatoh body** in a custom **Copper Age** finish, tortoiseshell pickguard
- **Maple neck**, "C" profile, **25.5" scale**
- **Two Fender-designed Alnico Telecaster single-coils**
- Standard Tele controls — volume, tone, three-way selector
- **12-string top-load / string-through bridge**
- Custom **Telecaster XII headstock**

It's derived from Van Leeuwen's own Paul Waller Custom Shop Masterbuilt 12-string Tele — the guitar you've been hearing on Queens of the Stone Age records, and before that on A Perfect Circle and Failure, without necessarily knowing that's what it was.

## The Quote Is the Whole Story

Van Leeuwen said this about it: *"When creating my second signature with Fender, I wanted an instrument at an affordable price point that could inspire anyone who's ever been curious about playing a 12-string."*

That is not marketing filler, and I want to sit on it for a second, because I have a personal stake in this one.

My dad plays bajo sexto. It's a 12-string Mexican guitar built for conjunto, and it's the instrument I learned my first chords on in our living room in San Antonio when I was twelve. So my introduction to guitar was a twelve-string, and I know exactly what makes people put them down: **they go out of tune constantly, and restringing them is miserable.** Twelve strings is twelve tuning events, twelve chances for a course to drift, and a bridge that fights you every time one breaks.

The spec sheet answers both. The **top-load/string-through bridge** is the detail that Premier Guitar flagged as "player-friendly," and that's the right read — a top-load option means you're feeding strings in from the back of the bridge instead of threading twelve of them through a body, which turns a forty-minute job into a ten-minute one. And a proper 12-string bridge with individual saddle adjustment is what keeps the octave courses actually intonated instead of approximately intonated.

Those are unglamorous specs. They're also the difference between a 12-string you play and a 12-string that lives in a case.

## What Actually Changes in Your Patch

Here's the part the announcement doesn't cover, and it's the part that matters if you bring one of these home to a modeler rig like mine.

**A 12-string is already a chorus pedal.** The octave courses are never perfectly in tune with each other — that's the whole sound, that shimmer is detuning — and the moment you add a chorus block on top, you get seasick warble instead of jangle. My honest advice: **turn the chorus off and start from dry.** If you want more movement, add it back at a depth that would be almost inaudible on a six-string. Our [Robert Smith chorus tone guide](/blog/cure-robert-smith-chorus-tone) is a great read for what chorus is *for* — and a 12-string is the one guitar where you should read it and then do less of it.

**Compression matters more than usual, and differently.** Twelve strings hit the pickup with a lot more transient energy than six, and the octave strings decay faster than the fundamentals. A slow-attack compressor lets the initial clang through and then holds up the octaves as they fade, which is exactly what you want. A fast, squashed country setting flattens the sparkle you paid for. Start around a 3:1 ratio with the attack backed off, and check it against our [compressor pedal settings guide](/blog/compressor-pedal-settings-guide) — the section on attack time is the one that applies here.

**Watch your gain, and watch your low mids.** Twelve strings into medium gain turns to mud fast, because you now have two sources in the same low-mid region for every note in the chord. Van Leeuwen's records are a good tell — the 12-string parts are mostly clean-to-edge-of-breakup with the dirt coming from somewhere else in the arrangement. If you want the guitar to bite, get it from the bridge Alnico single-coil and a touch of drive, not from cranking the amp block. And if the chords are turning to soup, the fix is usually a high-pass around 100 Hz rather than less gain.

**Two single-coils and a three-way is a feature, not a limitation.** This is a Tele. The neck pickup is the shimmering, harmonically dense sound people buy 12-strings for; the bridge is the cutting jangle that sits on top of a mix. On a modeler you can stop thinking about "a 12-string preset" and start thinking about two: a neck-pickup clean-with-space patch and a bridge-pickup edge-of-breakup patch. Our [dream pop clean chain order](/blog/dream-pop-clean-chain-order-chorus-delay-modulated-reverb) piece is the closest thing we have to a blueprint for the first one.

## And If You're Not Buying One

You can get partway there without spending $600, and I say this as somebody whose entire rig is a Jazzmaster and a Stomp on the coffee table.

A 12-string simulation on a modeler is really three things stacked: a **pitch block set an octave up, mixed well under the dry signal** (not at unity — somewhere in the 30–40% range), a **very slight detune** on that octave voice so it doesn't sound synthetic, and a **short delay of 10–20 ms** on the octave path so the two "strings" don't arrive at the same instant. That last one is the trick most people skip, and it's the one that makes it sound like a guitar instead of a harmonizer.

It won't fool anybody who's held a real one. It gets you 70% of the way there for the cost of two blocks, which — for those of us who get twenty minutes on a Tuesday and don't have room for another guitar — is often the right trade. That's the same math I run in [the 20-minute practice session](/blog/20-minute-practice-session): constraints aren't the enemy, they're just the terms of the deal.

But if you've been curious about 12-strings and the thing stopping you is that they seem fussy and expensive, that's exactly the player Van Leeuwen said he built this for. At $599 with a bridge designed so restringing doesn't ruin your Saturday, the excuse got noticeably thinner.
